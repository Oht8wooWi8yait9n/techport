(Arabidopsis Suberin Mutants) Evaluating Plant Suberin Mutants for Enhanced Water and Nutrient Uptake to Increase Biomass Production Under Elevated CO2 Concentrations

NASA TechPort Project ID: 105645
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

Spaceflight cabin environments (ISS) typically have elevated CO2 which can affect plant growth and development. This report seeks to answer (1) Do plant roots with higher suberin content increase plant water use efficiency; (2) Can plant roots with lower suberin content produce higher biomass compared to plants with normal root suberin levels; and (3) Does growth under elevated CO2 concentrations and altered suberin content in roots have an additive effect on biomass production and mineral content?

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

Elevated CO2 leads to a decrease in stomatal conductance of approximately 20%, which reduces whole-plant water use, conserves soil moisture, and prevents the onset of drought stress that would otherwise reduce yields. Thus, higher CO2 can increase water use efficiency by both lowering stomatal conductance and increasing net biomass at the whole-plant scale.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Recent findings from FACE (Free-Air CO 2 Enrichment) and climate change studies have shown that plants grown at elevated (~ 700 ppm) CO 2 contain reduced levels of essential elements such as nitrogen, zinc, and iron. We hypothesize that spacecraft environments with elevated CO 2 might likewise result in less nutritious crops for human consumption. For elevated CO 2 treatment in the Environmental Test Chambers (ETC), plants were grown with CO 2 concentrations at either ambient or elevated levels (800 ± 25, 1600 ± 25, 4000 ± 25 ppm). Briefly, each 75 mm round pot was filled with pre-moistened Growing Mix without compaction and placed inside a plastic tray with troughs. Each pot was seeded with three to four seeds. Trays containing growing pots were covered with a plastic dome for seed germination. Once germinated, pots were transferred into individual ETCs for growth under ambient or elevated CO 2 conditions. Plants were grown for 30 days post-germination. At 30 days, rosettes were harvested and their fresh weight was recorded. The tissue was flash frozen into liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C until further use. Dried and ground plant material (0.2–0.5 g) was digested in an open vessel system using a graphite-heating block. The plant material was digested at 95°C using a modification of the protocol by the Environmental Protection Agency. A 5 mL aliquot of 70% nitric acid was added to the samples and then boiled for approximately two hours (or until sample was completely clear). After cooling 2.5 mL of 30%, hydrogen peroxide was applied. When the peroxide reaction ceased, samples were reheated for an additional 50 minutes in covered vials. Samples were cooled overnight, diluted to 50 mL with ultra-pure DI water and then passed through a 25 mm 0.45 µm syringe filter. Samples were analyzed via Inductively Coupled Optical Emission Spectrometry. A multi-element standard was diluted to the same acid concentration as the samples and quantification was done by external calibration. Water use efficiency of a plant relates water transpired to biomass produced and is an important parameter for the assessment of the impact of reductions in transpiration on plant productivity. We assessed effect of root suberin content on the water use efficiency of wild type (WT – Arabidopsis plants), enhanced suberin-1 loss-of-function mutation ( esb1 ) and hydroxylase of root suberized tissue knockout mutation ( horst1 ) plants. Our data showed that esb1 had higher water use efficiency compared to WT plants and no significant difference in the fresh weights for esb1 compared to WT plants at harvest. When compared to WT plants, esb1 were able to produce comparable biomass using less amounts of water. Elevated root suberin in esb1 has been shown to be associated with a root dependent increase in time to wilting during water stress. Resistance to wilting can be achieved via a reduction in water loss to the environment, from both the root and leaves, or through an increase in the ability to take up water from the soil. Therefore, our results showed that increasing root suberin content could help plants tolerate drought stress considering plants grown on the ISS have plagued with over or under watering issues. Revisiting the first objective of the project, “Do plant roots with higher suberin content translate into increased water use efficiency?” and the answer is yes, for the Arabidopsis plant roots in this study, higher suberin content did translate into increased water use efficiency. To assess effects of root suberin content on total biomass production we grew all three plant types, WT, esb1 and horst1 under controlled environment conditions with ambient CO 2 i.e., 415 ± 25 ppm. After growing for 4-weeks and harvesting, we did not observe any significant difference in the total biomass accumulated by horst1 plants compared to WT, whereas, esb1 plants accumulated significantly (P-value Increased suberin content of the esb1 roots could create an extracellular transport barrier limiting apoplastic radial transport of water and solutes to above ground parts, which could explain overall lower biomass for these plants. On the other hand, horst1 has a patchy/discontinuous suberin deposition, which in theory should have allowed for unrestricted flow of water and nutrients to aboveground parts resulting in increased biomass compared to WT plants. HORST gene encodes for a ω-hydroxylases enzyme, which is required for suberin monomer biosynthesis but plants being sessile organisms, there have been numerous reports of redundant mechanisms in place for critical functions like these to ensure survivability, which could explain the absence of increased biomass compared to WT. Thus, increased root suberin content does not necessarily translate into increased biomass. To explore if there is a correlation between growth under elevated CO 2 concentrations and root suberin content, we grew all three genotypes under ambient and elevated (800 ± 25, 1600 ± 25, 4000 ± 25 ppm) CO 2 concentrations. Comparing growth of esb1 plants to WT under ambient and elevated CO 2 concentrations, esb1 always showed significantly (P-value horst1 mutant plants under 400 and 800 ppm appeared to be lower than the WT but no statistical difference was observed. Further, the growth differences nullified at 1600 and 4000 ppm. Comparing among themselves, all three genotypes showed gradual increase in total biomass through 1600 ppm before leveling off at 4000 ppm. Once again, this observation was found to be consistent with our earlier results. Elemental analysis of these plants under increasing CO 2 concentrations revealed a very interesting data. We analyzed leaf tissue samples from all three genotypes for changes in the contents of essential elements such as copper, potassium, magnesium, sodium, zinc, manganese, aluminum, calcium, iron, sulfur, boron and phosphorus as function of root suberin content. WT plants when grown at elevated CO 2 concentrations exhibited overall decrease in calcium, magnesium, manganese, phosphorus, potassium, sodium, sulfur and zinc content compared to a plants grown at ambient CO 2 . Plants with higher root suberin content ( esb1 ) showed a different behavior. Calcium and magnesium contents for esb1 plants decreased at all CO 2 concentrations, whereas for other metals (iron, manganese, phosphorus, potassium, sodium, molybdenum and zinc) 800 and 1600 ppm, generally showed an increase. Plants with mutation in the HORST gene ( horst1 ; less root suberin compared to WT) showed the most dramatic changes in the elemental composition. For most elements analyzed, horst1 showed an average increase of 30-60 % in the elemental composition around 1600 ppm before either decreasing or levelling off to WT. In order to gain further understanding into the differences in root suberin contents among WT, esb1 , horst1 and its implications on plant growth we carried out RNA sequencing of all three genotypes grown at increasing CO 2 concentrations. Comparing WT plants to esb1 , majority of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were categorized in biological processes such as response to nutrient levels, response to water stimulus and response to nitrogen levels. In the same comparison, maximum number of DEGs (436 genes) came from the ambient compared to 4000 ppm of CO 2 concentration. Again, the DEGs were categorized into biological processes such as the response to nutrient stress, response to external stimulus and anthocyanin biosynthetic and metabolic processes. WT plants compared to horst1 showed a similar observation with enrichment of gene ontology (GO) terms for biological processes related to response to external stimulus, response to water, and response to oxygen levels. This comparison showed enrichment of DEGs from biochemical pathways associated with cutin, suberin and wax biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis and lysine degradation. Overall, comparing WT to either esb1 or horst1 plants, the RNA sequencing revealed a common set of genes related to stress response, i.e., water uptake or nutrient availability stress (in both cases; WT vs. esb1 and WT vs. horst1 ) found to be upregulated especially during the exposure to 800 and 1600 ppm CO 2 . Prolonged exposure to 4000 ppm in all three genotypes had a comparable response in terms DEGs and GO term enrichment, which was mostly either flavonoid and anthocyanin biosynthetic processes or photosynthetic acclimation. This makes sense as Arabidopsis plants either reached their optimum productivity between 1600 and 4000 ppm but the prolonged exposure also caused some stress, which resulted in anthocyanin production. One of the noticeable group of DEG’s between esb1 and horst1 were the transmembrane transporters, which are mostly involved in metal transports across the cell membrane. Upregulation of these genes in horst1 compared to WT plants corroborates higher levels of calcium, magnesium and potassium found in the tissue samples. In conclusion, with this study we have shown that plants with lower root suberin content may or may not increase the total biomass but can definitely improve the nutritional (elemental) content of these plants. Secondly, roots with higher suberin content could improve water use efficiency and confer tolerance to drought stress. Both of these outcomes are important for growing food in spacecraft environment as, horst1 like modification to ‘Outredgeous’ lettuce can not only produce higher biomass at 3000 ppm (ISS levels) CO 2 but also make it nutritionally dense whereas esb1-like modification could increase its tolerance to drought stress.
