Flat Fabrication of Progressively Self-Assembling Space Systems

NASA TechPort Project ID: 106053
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

This project aims to study the feasibility of integrating an array of microchips with space satellite functions, or ChipSats, on a multifunctional shape memory polymer (SMP) bus that is capable of self-folding when exposed to solar radiation. This technology may enable the flat fabrication of kilometer-sized antenna arrays for radio astronomy that self-transform into the operative configuration once in orbit.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

Self-folding decouples final design from manufacturing and storage complexity. Lighter or cheaper than tethered or truss frameworks of the same size. No drifting dynamics or tight control requirements as for ChipSat swarms. Significantly more options for custom design of final geometry than structure obtained by alternate deformation mechanisms.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: This design report constitutes the overall mission concept and preliminary analyses and trade studies for the NIAC Phase 1 study “Flat Fabrication of Progressively Self Assembling Space Systems”. Beginning from the design philosophy, we briefly discussed the scientific motivation driving our mission concept, with the current application being one that enables us to deploy a kilometer sized interferometer array on-orbit to observe the Dark Ages signal. We then explored the existing trade space of spaceborne observation architectures, noting their advantages and disadvantages in regards to cost, complexity, and observation/interferometric performance capabilities. The preliminary technical design in this mission concept consisted of both theoretical and experimental work. Beginning with the design of the antenna array geometry, preliminary interferometer performance analyses resulted in the baseline target geometry of a 3 km diameter, 3 turn spiral comprised of 150 individual antennas. Kinematic simulations allowed us to visualize various folding laws for SMP deployment and generate a solution space encompassing the mapping of double spiral geometries; we also concluded that activating the folding behavior in a sequential manner is the most practical approach for this mission concept. Lastly, from reduced fidelity dynamics, we noted that the inclusion of an external spin torque in the direction opposing folding behavior may significantly aid the deployment process. Subsequent FEA analyses modeling our baseline target geometry provided further insights into maximum displacements of the overall, kilometer sized rigid structure. Experimental results based off lab data yielded insights into the torque, angular velocity, and angular acceleration temporal profiles, which can be integrated into the existing dynamical frameworks in future works. Additional lab experiments provided data regarding to UV radiation exposure, atomic oxygen exposure, deployment in microgravity, and determination of minimum energy input for SMP activation. Preliminary thermal analyses identified potential baseline orbits in cislunar space, along with possible optical coatings that provide the SNAIL concept with survivable temperature ranges. By appropriately defining the thermal network, node analyses was conducted over a variety of conditions. Beginning with a single node model, possible optical properties were chosen and used to develop a multi-model to produce and analyze a temperature gradient across the spacecraft. Trade studies and various numerical simulations allowed us to further assess, and address the intrinsic challenges in designing an antenna and onboard CDHS architecture to ensure that the observational data is appropriately stored and downlinked. Furthermore, the possibility of a heliocentric baseline orbit, rather than the currently assumed cislunar space, was considered as a possibly better alternative from a science operations perspective. Lastly, we explored the state of art in printable electronics, and the survivability of electronic components when subjected to the space environment and various forms of cosmic and galactic radiation. These analyses may provide further insights into material degradation of the SMP bus, and may strictly impose an upper limit in regards to mission lifetimes.
