Project Description

For arguably the first time, civilian applications are driving the innovation space for vertical lift. The onslaught of novel configurations for personal air vehicles, air taxis and even air buses has driven the traditional vertical lift community to reassess the design and analysis boundaries of the legacy rotorcraft vehicle. Electric and hybrid-electric propulsion systems introduce further design constraints with the addition of combinations of rotors-propellers-engines. These new paradigms indicate that the Advanced Air Mobility (AAM) vehicles need to meet a fundamentally different set of requirements. Developing insight in acquisition and operational cost as a function of operational use (maintenance and repair) is not only important but should be reversely accessible to steer the design of the vehicle as well as supply chain. Technology needs to deliver straightforward maintenance and repair techniques with thorough certification. It is critically important to ensure that safety requirements are met or exceeded, as a single catastrophic failure can have a devastating impact on the entire UAM community. The addition of new materials across the near to far terms of deployment must be assessed for their applicability in civil operations and civil business case models.

The technical challenge is to mitigate, and if possible, eliminate the technical barriers associated with the structures-related technology of vertical lift vehicles that make them less viable for civilian applications, while maintaining the highest safety standards. We are developing, validating, and demonstrating a novel, modular framework of analysis tools for operations, risk assessment, repair/maintenance, safety, and costs of AAM using current and advanced structures. Our unique team of university researchers, industry partners and maintenance experts have developed a combined numerical and experimental approach to develop an engineering- and science-based pc-based framework for operators and designers. This framework will permit to assess when maintenance and repair are needed using a low-cost monitoring system specific to local operations.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

Improved safety will minimizing costs for AAM operators are the major benefits of this research. New manufacturing/repair technology innovations on advanced composites including additive manufacturing and metamaterials will be transitioned to our industry partners and the US AAM community. A major goal of our ULI is to provide safety and maintenance analysis based on local environmental “wear and tear" rather than the traditional “one size fits all” schedule-based maintenance. These costs are a major barrier to meeting NASA’s Roadmap for deployment of AAM.

Beyond the technical benefits, our focus is on the development of the next generation workforce for AAM across all disciplines from engineers to maintenance to support personnel. US Census and National Science Foundation (NSF) studies indicate that our 2030-2040 workforce will have to come from US rural areas if we are to meet the ambitious goals of AAM. The project includes a major effort between our education, state government, and industry partners to provide a sustainable three-prong outreach program to these areas, starting in the rural South (GA and SC), including teachers, parents and students. The rural cohort in the South includes a broad diversity, so the focus at the university level is to address socio-economic, first-generation college mentoring, and encouragement for graduate school through internship, cooperative education, undergraduate research opportunities and fellowships with our investigators.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: For arguably the first time, civilian applications are driving the innovation space for vertical lift. The onslaught of novel configurations for personal air vehicles, air taxis and even air buses has driven the traditional vertical lift community to reassess the design and analysis boundaries of the legacy rotorcraft vehicle. Unmanned air vehicles and drones from very small to large sizes have driven the design space to include lower Reynolds numbers than those in the traditional vertical lift community. Electric and hybrid-electric propulsion systems introduce further design constraints with the addition of combinations of rotors-propellers- engines. While many General Aviation (GA) aircraft are of similar size as those considered for Advanced or Urban Air Mobility (AAM or UAM), none of these were dedicated to urban flight and the utilization for those vehicles was much lower than for any of the AAM vehicles currently considered and under development. GA operational costs are not close to those envisioned for AAM. These new paradigms indicate that the AAM vehicles need to meet a fundamentally different set of requirements. Developing insight in acquisition and operational cost as a function of operational use (maintenance and repair) is not only important, but should be reversely accessible to steer the design of the vehicle as well as supply chain. Technology needs to deliver straightforward maintenance and repair techniques with thorough certification. It is critically important to ensure that safety requirements are met or exceeded, as a single catastrophic failure can have a devastating impact on the entire AAM community. The addition of new materials across the near to far terms of deployment must be assessed for their applicability in civil operations and civil business case models. AAM are defined in this proposal to include vehicles for passenger travel and cargo delivery, considering both piloted and autonomous options. The overarching research objective is to improve affordability and safety by developing simulation tools and operational processes for cost, safety, and performance of civilian vertical lift air vehicles and transitioning successful university research in advanced materials. A modular framework of operational analysis tools, new processes, and new techniques has been developed so that NASA and the AAM community can apply them to other current and future concepts, both in structures and other maintenance/repair areas of interest. The team, comprised of university researchers, industry engineers, and maintenance faculty, executed a novel interdisciplinary, highly interwoven approach to develop the methods needed to design civilian vehicles with vertical lift capability that incorporate advanced structural concepts while including the constraints necessary for a successful AAM operation. These project objectives contribute significantly to advance NASA Aeronautics Research Mission Directorate (ARMD) MegaDriver 1: Global Growth in Demand for High Speed Mobility via Strategic Thrust 4: Safe, Quiet and Affordable Vertical Lift Air Vehicles. In particular, the research addressed the range of advances needed for N+2 to post N+3 Levels for the Research Theme Safe and Certifiable Vertical Takeoff and Landing (VTOL) Technologies. Four Technical Challenges (TC) were tackled using a translational approach that included a partnership of personnel whose expertise ranges from basic research to maintenance and manufacturing. Specifically, this research developed operating cost and safety operator-friendly models unique to the local environment (TC1) in an overarching integrated framework (TC4) that are informed by new material technologies that improve and ensure safe maintenance and repair (TC2) while extending the maintenance interval safely and providing feedback for operations that can impact comfort and noise (TC3). These aid the NASA short-term goals (2025) by overcoming cost and safety barriers of point-to-point air transportation and provide an expandable framework for new and future technologies as they transition to sufficient TRL (2035).
