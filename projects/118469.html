Silicon Carbide Power Components for NASA Lunar Surface Applications

NASA TechPort Project ID: 118469
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio

Project Description

The low losses of silicon carbide (SiC) power diodes and transistors, plus their high voltage and current ratings, make them ideal for power conversion and control in spacecraft and on lunar and martian bases. Heavy-ion radiation tests of SiC power diodes and MOSFETs (Metal–Oxide–Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistors) simulating the space radiation environment reveal two unexpected behaviors: 1) Off-state current has discrete increases with each ion strike, and 2) A catastrophic failure, single-event-burnout (SEB) where off-state current of the transistor or diode increases suddenly to destructive levels following an ion strike can occur at voltages significantly below maximum voltage rating. The SEB failures damage the device permanently and must be eliminated for SiC power devices to reliably work in NASA space applications.

Our team will deliver radiation-tolerant SiC power diodes and transistors that can operate near rated voltages without radiation-induced burnout or degradation in order to realize the advantages in size, weight, power loss, and volume that SiC devices have for space. Our approach will involve radiation testing at a national ion accelerator facility, device and circuit analysis using appropriate simulation tools, and device design and fabrication. We will initiate this work using state-of-the art SiC power transistors and diodes from GE that approximate the required specifications for the LuSTR program element. Then, following heavy ion test and analysis, next generation devices that will meet the LuSTR specifications will be designed, fabricated by GE, and tested against specifications in ion beams by Vanderbilt researchers.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

The SiC power devices have the potential to significantly reduce size, weight, power loss, and volume of electronic power converters in spacecraft compared to silicon-based power converters. This project examines how DC power lines can be operated at much higher voltages with SiC than with silicon, further increasing power efficiency. Additionally, the SiC devices may be used in spacecraft with novel power systems, for example nuclear fission.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Problem Statement and Objectives Silicon carbide (SiC) power transistors are much more efficient than silicon power devices, especially for high voltage applications. This LuSTR project addressed the problem that silicon carbide power diodes and transistors with high voltage ratings experience destructive “Single Event Burnout” (SEB) from ions in space at low bias voltages. In prior work, many researchers worldwide had shown that a SiC diode or MOSFET rated at 1200 V in the off state can experience SEB at voltages as low as 500 V, depending on the energy and type of ion. Allowing for design margin, this SEB behavior means that SiC power devices rated for 1200 V operation on earth could only be used in a 350 V power system in space. There is also a slow degradation process caused by ions in SiC devices, called “Single Event Leakage Current,” (SELC), in which each ion traversing the SiC device causes a small but permanent change in the off-state leakage current of the device. The SELC causes SiC devices to exceed their specified off-state leakage current after some time in a space ion radiation environment. In addition to increasing the SEB threshold, we sought to understand and decrease the SELC that SiC devices would see in space. The LuSTR call for proposals stated a goal of a 1200 V SEB threshold for diodes and a 600 V SEB threshold for MOSFETS, up to LET of 40 MeV-cm2/mg. The intended application for these SiC power devices is to control an electrical power grid on the moon. Approach The 2020 LuSTR project entitled “Silicon Carbide Power Components for NASA Lunar Surface Applications,” proposed by Vanderbilt University and their fabrication partner General Electric Aerospace, sought to improve (increase) the off-state voltage at which SEB occurred in SiC devices while maintaining electrical performance that was better than comparable silicon devices. The proposed approach was to look at at higher-voltage power diodes and transistors from GE that has a thicker epitaxial region than 1200 V SiC devices, but still much thinner than equivalent silicon devices. (The epitaxial region, or “epi,” in a vertical device is a low-doped region that allows the device to block or maintain a high voltage in the off state.) The proposed plan was to measure the SEB behavior of some 3300 V “baseline” SiC power devices that GE had already fabricated at an ion beam facility at Texas A&M University (TAMU) to see if their SEB threshold was different from 1200 V devices. Once the baseline radiation performance was established, the team could design and fabricate new radiation-tolerant SiC devices with changes to their structure intended to improve the SEB threshold to 1200 V for diodes and 600 V for MOSFETs, and measure the new SiC devices at TAMU. The resulting change in SEB and SELC would provide insight into the physical mechanisms involved and how to change future SiC devices to optimize their hardness to ion radiation effects in space. Another aspect of the approach was to use imaging techniques, namely electroluminescence and photoluminescence (EL and PL) to identify physical changes to the SiC device materials during exposure to the ion beam to understand the material science basis of the observed radiation behavior.
