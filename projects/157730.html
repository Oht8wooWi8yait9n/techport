Project Description

Exploration missions beyond low Earth orbit will experience significant challenges in crew medical care. Among these challenges are the exposure of provided medications to vacuum conditions. The impacts of environmental exposures of vacuum on medications and packaging are not fully characterized. Vehicles supporting the Artemis missions will have dedicated, vehicle-specific medical kits as well as crew medical accessory kits. Although most of the kits will be stored in a pressurized, climate-controlled volume, there are scenarios in which they may become exposed to vacuum. In addition, Mars missions will have limited re-supply missions which will result in extended storage of available medications. In most cases the shelf lives of the supplied medications are less than the duration of an expected Mars mission.  

The Exploration Medical Integrated Product Team (XMIPT) in collaboration with the Department of Defense is conducting a study to assess the effects of vacuum on solid and liquid medications and their packaging to be used in exploration missions. The medications for this study were identified based on those currently being considered for future Artemis missions and represent the types of pharmaceuticals and formulations that are likely to comprise an exploration formulary. The study was conducted in two phases, with Phase A evaluating exposure in the manufacturer’s packaging. In Phase B the solid medications were exposed to vacuum without packaging, and the fluids in their manufacturer’s packaging. Phase B medications were analyzed for their Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API) at timepoints 0, 3, 6 and 9 months. The study manuscript is draft.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

The results from the pharmacy vacuum study will likewise aid in decision making related to the development of medical kits, medication selection and packaging and stowage for long duration Lunar and Mars missions and inform the direction of future medication in vacuum studies.

Phase B medications were analyzed for their Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API) at timepoints 0, 3, 6 and 9 months. In FY24 Phase A, exposure of the medications in their manufacturer’s packaging was conducted with results received January 2024. To test package integrity, 12 solid medications and 10 medications in fluid forms were exposed to vacuum at depress rates of 1 psi/hr and 7psi/hr and held for one hour. Effects of vacuum exposure in Phase A included no observations of gross package failures. However, there were reversible changes such as ballooning which were limited to the bottles. Preliminary results of Phase A were presented at the 2024 Aerospace Medical Association Annual Scientific Meeting. Phase B focused on understanding degradation in medications exposed to vacuum, with exposure in June 2024. In this second phase, 19 medications were exposed without packaging for solids and in manufacturers packaging for fluids, to a vacuum at a rate of 1pis/hr and held for 8 hours. Through FY 24 and FY 25, API testing was conducted at 0-, 3-, 6- and 9-month timepoints. Minimal ( This study provides the first controlled, longitudinal dataset isolating the effect of simulated vacuum on pharmaceuticals relevant to operations in the exploration environment. Findings indicate that short-duration near-vacuum exposure has limited impact on most medications, with only specific formulations demonstrating sensitivity. In conclusion, simulated vacuum exposure for up to eight hours produced minimal impact on the stability of most tested pharmaceuticals over nine months of storage. The results of Phase B were presented at the 2025 Aerospace Medical Association Annual Scientific Meeting, and a study manuscript prepared for submission. In FY25, the Medications in a Vacuum Study closed with the submission of a final report “Determining Pharmaceutical Stability after Exposure to Hypobaric Space Environments.” The findings from this pilot study will aid in decision making related to the development of medical kits, medication selection and packaging and stowage for long duration lunar and Mars missions and inform the direction of future medication in vacuum studies.
