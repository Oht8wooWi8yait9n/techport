Pathway toward high-efficiency III-V/Si tandem solar cells

NASA TechPort Project ID: 118488
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio

Project Description

Developing high-efficiency, low-cost solar cell technology is essential to both enabling a wider variety of space missions and making solar energy competitive with fossil fuels. One promising cell design is to integrate a III-V top cell onto an inexpensive Si bottom cell to form a tandem (dual junction). In particular, a GaAsP/Si tandem cell has an optimal material combination to achieve theoretical efficiencies of 37-44%, well above the 26.7% of record Si cells. Unfortunately, GaAsP cells grown on Si suffer from the formation of performance-robbing threading dislocations due to the large lattice constant mismatch between GaAsP and Si (~3.2%). These dislocations lead to poor diffusion lengths of light-generated electrons and holes, reducing the short-circuit current density (Jsc). To increase the Jsc and overall efficiency of the GaAsP cell, I propose to grow a thin GaAsP solar cell on top of a distributed Bragg reflector (DBR). The DBR reflects photons that would otherwise transmit through the GaAsP cell, giving a “second chance” for the photons to be absorbed and generate additional electron-hole pairs. Since the DBR effectively doubles the optical thickness, the GaAsP cell can also be thinner, mitigating the issues associated with low diffusion length due to defects. Early simulation work predicts a ~1.5mA/cm2 increase in Jsc and 1.5% higher cell efficiency with a DBR. I will carry out significant experimental work to grow the DBR’s, refine simulation models and improve DBR design. I will also address potential challenges of dislocation formation in the DBR and devise strategies to reduce the cost of the additional DBR growth. Ultimately, I aim to increase the GaAsP cell efficiency from the current record of 16.5% to >20%. This will enable the development of low-cost GaAsP/Si tandem cells with >30% efficiency and widespread applications for both space and terrestrial use.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Photovoltaic (PV) or solar cells are an important source of power generation for both terrestrial and space applications. For terrestrial applications, single-junction (SJ) silicon (Si) solar cells have dominated the market due to their low cost, high-efficiency and reliability. Space applications primarily use multi-junction solar cells (MJSC) grown from III-V compound semiconductors, which can achieve higher efficiencies than SJ Si and have greater tolerance to electron/proton irradiation above the earth’s atmosphere. Due to the high launch costs and low production volumes of space PV, the efficiency and radiation hardness advantages of III-V MJSCs have outweighed their ~100× higher production costs in dollars per watt compared to Si. However, reducing the cost of space PV will become more important as launch costs decrease and total PV output increases in large-scale applications such as satellite constellations. Much of the high III-V cell cost is due to the use of expensive Ge or GaAs substrates for cell growth. For these substrates, a key approach to reducing cost is substrate reuse, in which a cell is etched and peeled from the substrate, while the substrate surface is polished and etched for subsequent growths, adding significant processing complexity. An alternative approach is the growth of III-V on inexpensive Si substrates, with the Si acting as the “bottom cell” of the MJSC. Such III-V/Si MJSCs have theoretical efficiencies of > 40%, and have experimentally achieved record efficiencies of 35.9% [1], [2], much higher than the 26.8% record for Si [3]. Direct III-V on Si growth of solar cells has advantages in simple cell processing and integration into PV modules. However, a significant disadvantage is the lattice mismatch of 3-4% for III-V materials of interest to Si, such as GaAsyP1-y and GaxIn1-xP. This lattice mismatch leads to the formation of extended defects to relieve strain; defects known as misfit dislocations form along the growth interfaces and perpendicular to growth direction, and are terminated by threading dislocations, which can propagate in the growth direction of the III-V layers. The threading dislocations are harmful to III-V solar cells since they can act as non-radiative recombination centers that degrade cell efficiency. Several approaches have been developed to minimize threading dislocation density (TDD), such as the use of dislocation filter layers (DFL) and thermal cycle annealing to encourage dislocation movement and annihilation and compositionally graded buffers (CGB) that gradually relieve strain with a defined strain rate (%/𝜇𝑚) and maximize relaxation through misfits rather than threading dislocations. A target TDD of less than 1×106 cm-2 is calculated to have minimal detrimental effects on III-V solar cells [4]. In the Lee group, TDD as low as 4×106 cm-2 has been achieved with GaAsP CGB [5], though more typical achievable values are around 1×107 cm-2 [6].
