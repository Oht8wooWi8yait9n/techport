Project Description

Understanding the beginnings of the Universe and life itself is NASA’s long term vision and one of humanities’ grand challenges. Missions to the edge of our solar system and to space between stars in our galaxy – the interstellar medium – are of a great promise to shed light on these questions. However, today’s deep space exploration is hindered by fundamental limitations of current propulsion technology, resulting in missions that take decades of development, years of flight and cost billions. Extreme metamaterial solar sails as proposed here have the potential to shift the paradigm of space exploration enabling numerous low cost and high speed missions to be launched anytime and anywhere. Such sails could gain accelerations >60AU/yr when coupled to low mass spacecraft and dive to extreme proximity to the sun (just 2-5 solar radii). This velocity is 20 times more than Voyager 1. This NIAC study challenges the limits of materials, paving the way for development of high endurance ultra-thin film architectures that can handle extreme environments manifested by solar radiation and plasma in addition to providing spacecraft control. Our technology enables reaching Jupiter in 5 months, Neptune in 10, surpassing Voyager 1 in 2.5 years and getting to the solar gravity lens location in just 8.5 years.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

Current deep space missions are slow and expensive. Our sail-crafts are >20 times faster and cheaper. Our sail technology will yield novel spacecraft design and CONOPS, enabling distributed deep space missions, constellations & assembly of large structures. Beyond solar sail technology, our extreme metamaterials will find use for ultralight space structures and for in-space power and thermal management. The concepts and findings of our work will find use in plasma resilient materials, high temperature coatings, and radiative heat damping.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Our Phase I study has examined use of extreme metamaterial solar sails to enable ultra-fast, accessible and scalable exploration of deep space. Specifically, we have studied the feasibility of a powered slingshot via an unprecedentedly close perihelion approach (≤6R_☼) to propelling a sailcraft to 60AU/yr heliocentric exit velocity (or 0.1% of the speed of light). A particular focus of our program was on materials that can enable such an extreme solar sailing. In addition to materials study, we have examined other elements of extreme solar sailing, including spacecraft architecture, solar environment, and concept of operation, as well as identified key areas for technology development. Our Phase I effort demonstrates that the envisioned breakthrough solar sailing is physically and technologically feasible with a proper development of supporting technologies. Sail materials. We have identified sail materials as a major challenge of the mission concept. Our analysis of the solar environment shows that sunlight (>7 MW/m2 at 3R_☼) absorption with subsequent heating to high temperatures is a major limiting factor governing sail materials design. We have surveyed 25 material candidates and identified silicon nitride (SiN) and titanium nitride (TiN) as best constituent materials for solar sail design based on their melting temperatures (>2000K), optical properties, and low density. We examined sail material designs based on both of these materials. Our SiN based sail designs present nanostructured thin film metamaterials with ~0.7g/m^2 and ~80% reflectance with ~0.1% of sunlight absorption. Such sails can get to 3R☼ perihelion and can maintain temperature 1800K). Such sails reflect ~60% of sunlight absorbing the rest (~40%). As a prototype we fabricated sub-micron thick graphene oxide films coated with 30 nm TiN. During fabrication a new method of creating smooth ultrathin films was implemented. In Phase I we proposed and studied two mission concepts: Fast Transit Interstellar Probe (getting to 500 AU in 10 years) and Corona-Net (formation flying for heliophysics science at 60 AU/yr necessitates ~100x100 m2 sail, suggesting that an ultralight sail support architecture is needed. Preliminary study indicates that such sail systems are feasible, provided necessary technology development. Sail controls. Together with sail materials we have examined sail control scenarios. Our analysis shows that at close perihelion approaches, due to a high solar flux and large sail area, high torques are induced. We further examined a new control approach based on the use of ablative materials. For all scenarios we find that a 0 deg to 90 deg turn may be accomplished in less than 5 mins.
