Project Description

The ISRU Pilot Excavator (IPE) will be NASA's first lunar surface robot specifically designed with the reliability and efficiency to excavate large quantities of regolith. This capability is critical to sustained lunar mission success. IPE is targeting development of a full TRL 6 robotic excavator system in FY27. To accomplish this, the IPE project will develop a 30 kg-class excavator to demonstrate robotic excavation of large amounts (10,000 kg) of granular lunar regolith during a future lunar demonstration. IPE uses novel excavation tools, called bucket drums, which are hollow cylinders with scoops staggered around the outside. Regolith is collected with the scoops and flows into the drum where it is captured by an internal baffle system. The excavator can then transport the regolith in the drum and reverse the direction of the drum rotation to dispense the regolith back out. IPE uses two sets of bucket drums that dig simultaneously in opposing directions and results in counter-acting excavation forces. This combination of bucket drum excavation tools and counter-acting excavation forces enables low mass robotic excavators to effectively dig in reduced gravity environments. This is a significant departure from terrestrial excavators that rely on high mass to produce tractive forces to counteract the forces of excavation. IPE will traverse 100m between each digging and dumping operation to reflect the needs of future ISRU plants that want to source regolith a minuimum distance from the lander to avoid contamination. Additional novel features include camera modules with integreated electro-dynamic dust shields, a wireless battery recharging system, and a dust tolerant thermal control system.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

The ISRU Pilot Excavator project will include a ground demonstration of ISRU-relevant scale excavation in a simulated lunar environment. The outcome of this project will be an excavation system with Technolgy Readiness Level of 6 demonstrating pilot-scale excavation and providing confidence for future investments into ISRU Pilot Plant hardware. The data from the ground demonstration will be used to inform follow-on excavation systems and a development of a new class of "blue collar" planetary robot . This data will include, but is not limited to, the performance of the excavation tools, abrasive wear on critical surfaces, impacts of dust exposure, and the condition of regolith after repeated trafficking. To support a follow on ISRU Pilot Plant demonstration, appropriate design updates based on lessons learned and ISRU direction will be made. Future iterations of robotic excavators may include additional capabilities such as increased autonomous navigation, lunar night survivability, icy regolith excavation, and construction tasks.​

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The ISRU Pilot Excavator (IPEx) project was initiated with the goal of developing NASA's first dedicated lunar surface robot, specifically engineered to excavate and deliver large quantities of lunar regolith to support future in-situ resource utilization (ISRU) missions. The plan called for bridging a critical technology gap: creating a flight-ready system capable of excavating 10,000 kg of regolith to enable pilot-scale resource extraction, such as oxygen production. The team designed a 30 kg-class excavator that utilized innovative counter-rotating bucket drum mechanisms, dust-tolerant thermal and actuator systems, autonomous navigation, and wireless charging interfaces. These systems were developed and tested extensively, with all major objectives and requirements successfully reviewed and advanced through TRL 5, including long-duration autonomous demonstrations and subsystem ground testing.The accomplishments of the IPEx project stand out as significant advancements for robotic excavation in lunar environments. The team validated the scalability and reliability of the bucket drum design, completed critical trade studies, released public autonomy datasets, and performed mobility and dust-mitigation testing. Autonomous mock missions demonstrated repeated excavation, regolith delivery, and navigation capabilities over more than 58 km of simulated traverses. Industry and agency partnerships, such as the collaboration with Caterpillar for verification of autonomy and mobility simulations, and a successful suborbital flight test with Blue Origin, strengthened the project's deliverables and technology validation. The project culminated with the creation of two qualification units and the storage of a flight-ready system for future missions.Industry infusion and outreach have been central to the IPEx effort, with proactive dissemination of results and technology to the wider space and robotics communities. The IPEx team has engaged in robust outreach by presenting at major conferences including LSIC, AIAA ASCEND, IEEE Aerospace, and the Space Resources Roundtable, and by supporting NASA events such as GCD APR and NASA Day on the Hill. The project has also fostered STEM engagement with initiatives like FIRST Robotics. Commercial partners, including Starpath Robotics, Blue Origin, Lockheed Martin, and Interlune, are utilizing IPEx-derived technology, and the counter-rotating bucket drum concept has been licensed to 11 companies for terrestrial and space applications. These efforts have not only accelerated the path to lunar surface operations, but have also ingrained IPEx technology in the landscape of future missions, Artemis campaign planning, and commercial lunar infrastructure.
