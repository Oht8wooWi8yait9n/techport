Project Description

The risks and challenges of the space environment have logically led to proposals to use robots to perform tasks for efficiency and safety reasons. Robotic free flyers (autonomous or controllable robots that operate in microgravity local to a host) have multiple ongoing research areas including autonomous docking systems, Visual Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) of space environments, characterizing control methods for docked/connected bodies, and the application of these areas to various practical situations. The ongoing development needed to increase the technology readiness level (TRL) of robotic free flyers promises to allow for the performance of tasks previously delegated to scarce astronaut on-orbit time, increase mission safety, and even lay the groundwork for on-orbit assembly and servicing techniques. Free flyer research is largely constrained by the testbeds available. The significance of recent upgrades to the MIT SPHERES satellites onboard the ISS is not overstated in noting that they provide the world's first reconfigurable, on-orbit testbed of multi-satellite docking. Relatedly, research into free flying space robotic manipulators could well be applied to the SPHERES testbed. Applications of manipulators to free flyers include the ability to "perch" to prevent free floating. The NASA technology roadmaps point to the development of robotic mobility and manipulation systems (TABS 4.2 and 4.3). Recognizing the value of the current configuration of the Halo-enabled SPHERES testbed and interest in satellite/free flyer manipulation activities, my proposed research aims to investigate areas of improvement in free flyer manipulation capability, and its application in enhancing both IVA and EVA mobility. Specifically, this research can be broken down into the following 3 objectives: 1. Drive Development of the MIT ARM or Similar Free Flyer Manipulators 2. Design Algorithms for Useful IVA/EVA Applications of Free Flyer Manipulators, with an Emphasis on "Perched" Activity 3. Validate Free Flyer Manipulator Systems through Ground and On-Orbit Test Scenarios The goal of this study is to investigate manipulator designs for both perching and grappling manipulators, creating the control algorithms necessary for their use in docking and object manipulation, and testing their success in ground analogues and on-orbit. Suitable robotic manipulators will be developed to fulfill both the perching and grappling roles, followed by ground tests verifying a set of control laws for maneuvers that have applicability in free flyer mobility and EVA/IVA manipulation tasks. Addressing NASA technology needs, this investigation further offers the opportunity to expand on-orbit construction capability, assist with astronaut EVA/IVA activities, and increase the overall efficiency and safety of spaceflight operations.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

Suitable robotic manipulators will potentially expand on-orbit construction capability, assist with astronaut EVA/IVA activities, and increase the overall efficiency and safety of spaceflight operations.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Robotic space systems are increasingly moving toward higher levels of autonomy in search of greater productivity, safety, and operation in teleoperation-denied situations. Coupled with the availability of capable processors, a number of robotics technologies like onboard SLAM (simultaneous localization and mapping), MPC (model predictive control), and more are within computational reach. However, in the context of robotic planning and control, system uncertainties are often not fully considered for new mission scenarios of interest, like autonomous rendezvous with tumbling targets or on-orbit cargo maneuvering, for example. "Enhancing Docking and Manipulation Capability for Microgravity Robotic Free-Flyers" has focused on developing key rendezvous and post-grapple manipulation planning and control capabilities for microgravity close proximity operations under uncertainty. Namely, this work has focused on motion planning solutions that are information-aware and robust to inherent uncertainties while remaining real-time and near-optimal. That is, this work has applied concepts from robust and adaptive control, information-aware trajectory optimization, and real-time motion planning to provide motion plans that can allow a microgravity agent to learn about itself and its environment while providing certain performance guarantees during motion. The algorithmic goals of this work included: (1) planning under uncertainty to learn about system dynamics parameters while simultaneously achieving goal-oriented motion; (2) providing robust control solutions for prevailing levels of uncertainty; (3) applying and tailoring these algorithmic approaches to specific microgravity close proximity operations scenarios of interest to NASA. The resulting technologies developed are an information-aware motion planning framework to combine learning about robotic system unknowns with real-time robust model updates (RATTLE), and an autonomous rendezvous pipeline to enable close proximity rendezvous with unknown tumbling targets with control robustness guarantees. These approaches have been demonstrated through a simulation to hardware pipeline, including multiple on-orbit demonstrations of both technologies using NASA’s Astrobee robots---the first such low-level autonomy payload to run on the Astrobee robots. The approaches have demonstrated motion planning that is able to weigh parametric model learning in a real-time framework for robotic free-flyers for the first time, while simultaneously permitting real-time model and robustness updates---a valuable contribution that introduces control robustness guarantees that can adapt to the latest understanding of a system’s uncertainty. The autonomous rendezvous pipeline is under continued development by ESA/DLR for TRL maturation to reach and repair the tumbling EnviSat satellite; portions of the RATTLE framework are being infused into NASA’s Astrobee robot to provide robust control and possibly to power future caretaker robots aboard a lunar space station. Additionally, the work has resulted in a number of contributions to the Astrobee platform itself, including a guest scientist flight software guide and a soon-to-be-released open source testing framework for on-orbit autonomy experiments.
