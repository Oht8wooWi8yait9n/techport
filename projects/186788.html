Origami-inspired lattice architectures for robotic assembly and reconfiguration of modular space structures

NASA TechPort Project ID: 186788
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

This project addresses the challenge of assembling and maintaining large space structures using autonomous robotic systems. Future missions to the Moon and Mars will rely on robots to construct habitats, communication towers, and deployable arrays in environments characterized by uncertainty, limited sensing, and minimal human oversight. While recent advances in modular robotic assembly enable scalable construction, these systems remain mechanically static once assembled. Their stiffness and load-transfer behavior are fixed by geometry, requiring robots to compensate for uncertainty through increasingly precise sensing, planning, and control. The central objective of this research is to develop origami-inspired lattice architectures that enable robotic systems to assemble structures capable of mechanical reconfiguration after assembly. The project introduces cuboctahedral voxels with internally multistable mechanics, allowing individual modules to transition between discrete mechanical states in response to applied forces. These mechanically-encoded state machines enable behaviors such as self-locking, self-stiffening, and controlled reconfiguration without onboard actuation, changing part of the adaptation from software and sensing into the physical structure itself. The proposed research follows a three-stage approach. First, mechanically multistable voxel elements are designed using origami-inspired linkage geometries that produce discrete, stable configurations separated by energy barriers. Second, these designs are translated into physical hardware through the fabrication and testing of full-scale cuboctahedral modules with state-switching joints, enabling controlled mechanical reconfiguration. Third, robotic manipulation strategies are developed to place modules and trigger state transitions through force-controlled interaction, supported by vision-based perception and task-level reasoning. Physical prototypes and benchtop experiments are used throughout to evaluate performance under representative loading and assembly conditions. This work contributes new mechanical components, subsystems, and assembly frameworks that improve the robustness and autonomy of robotic surface operations. While machine learning methods such as vision--language--action models are incorporated to support perception and task execution, they are not the primary focus. Instead, learning-based tools complement a hardware-centered approach by enabling robots to reason over evolving structure geometry and contact conditions during assembly and reconfiguration. By enabling structures that adapt mechanically to uncertainty, this research supports NASA's objectives in autonomous robotics for surface logistics and sustainability, and advances early-stage technologies critical for long-duration, resilient robotic construction in space.
