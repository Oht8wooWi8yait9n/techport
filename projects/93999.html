Project Description

The Digital Astronaut Simulator (DAS) is a low Technology Readiness Level (TRL) NASA project with promising benefits to Human Health Countermeasures. In providing models for human-system interaction, it has the potential to influence the design of exercise devices for future space missions. It will also enable better understanding of physiological effects of performing the exercise motions. The aim of this project is to generate new methods and ideas for representing human motion accurately and implement them in a way that will enhance the TRL of the DAS and similar technologies. The creation of a robust, diverse biomechanics toolset will enhance and verify the DAS models through the integration of real kinematic, kinetic, and physiological data. Motion capture, force plate recordings, and pressure sensor measurements will be used to determine real motion trajectories and quantify aspects of human performance. Biosignal measuring devices will be used for redundancy in assessing human performance and also incorporated into correlation studies between the various biomechanics methods. A motion and virtual environment simulator will be used to add simulated external factors. The combined system of methods will reduce assumptions and reliance on estimations when creating human models. Data collection will be conducted with current exercise device designs in consideration. Once the models more accurately represent human interaction with exercise equipment already created, the DAS will then be more reliable for predicting use of future exercise equipment in the initial design stages. The project will also produce data necessary to model asymmetric motion and forces, which should be understood for better representation of human movement. Outcomes from this project will also enable new predictive models for human motion and physiological benefits. The biomechanics toolset that this project creates will advance the modeling and health countermeasure space technology areas by collecting data which answers ongoing questions. It will aid in solving concerns so as to further progress countermeasure efforts driven by deep space exploration mission goals.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Transitioned To (Florida Space Grant Consortium): "Investigation of Vibration Isolation System Design Parameters for Spaceflight Exercise Devices"
  • Closed Out: The Digital Astronaut Simulation sought enhancement by beginning to incorporate real data to verify and improve human biomechanical model analyses. Questions in human spaceflight involved the kinematics and dynamics of exercise with new exploration exercise devices and the development of a Vibration Isolation System (VIS). Motion capture and force data were collected in a range of environments and conditions to aid development in these areas and discover unknowns. This work explored the development of the Computer Assisted Rehabilitation Environment (CAREN) at the University of South Florida to function as a test environment for studying human-VIS interaction. A 6 Degree of Freedom (DOF) platform produced sinusoidal motion comparable to how a VIS would need to respond in the simplest, continuous, steady state exercise scenario for this proof-of-concept. The research studied exercises most in question such as the unconstrained row and squats, frequencies of exercise of greatest interest and concern, and the ability for those frequencies to be driven by external cues. All raw and processed data were shared with the Digital Astronaut Simulation team at the Johnson Space Center for use in their modeling and analyses. A sample of motion capture data from a trial in a parabolic flight test was fully processed to enable a comparison of two different motion capture techniques in flight conditions: Kinect depth sensing and multi-camera 3D reconstruction. Multiple tests were organized and facilitated by the research team: the first exercise test with motion capture at the Active Response Gravity Offload System (ARGOS) and the first motion capture collection of the seated row configuration for the Miniature Exercise Device (MED-2) in the Prototype Immersive Technology (PIT) laboratory. These collections demonstrated new application of technologies in use at NASA and provided useful data which can aid an increase in the technology readiness level of the Digital Astronaut Simulation and the efforts it supports, such as the design of a VIS.
