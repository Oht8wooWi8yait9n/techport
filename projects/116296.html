Project Description

As identified in the Artemis Plan, “Dust mitigation technologies that diminish dust hazards on lunar surface systems such as cameras, solar panels, spacesuits, and instrumentation" has been identified as a priority. This project seeks to develop advanced space mechanism bearing technologies for long-term sustained operation in lunar dust environments. The project will investigate and quantify the degree of dust infiltration in (grease-packed) space mechanism bearings using commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) and best practice sealing technologies, and it will identify and performance test advanced technologies/strategies to reduce dust infiltration and/or increase life expectancy of space mechanism bearings in the presence of lunar dust.

For long-life rotating mechanisms exposed to lunar regolith environments, bearings must provide sufficient resistance to particle infiltration, sufficiently high wear resistance, or both to meet mission requirements. The dust mitigation technologies' performance will be measured by the degree of dust infiltration and the degree of wear damage. Dust infiltration will be evaluated by measuring bearing torque, which can be shown to correspond well to intrusion of dust particles into bearings. Debris dent damage is characterized by permanent dents in the ball and/or the race of the bearing due to particulate in the lubricant. The debris dent damage will be measured with an optical microscope and quantified using image processing software to identify the maximum dent depth.

This project will develop novel testing equipment to evaluate space mechanism bearings in a lunar dust environment under ambient air conditions. Testing under lunar temperature and vacuum conditions will be an important next step after the conclusion of this project to assess the test data from a relevant environment.


Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

Sustained presence on the Moon will required increased lifetimes of sensitive mechanical joints and bearings for surface equipment such as EVA (Extra Vehicular Activity) suits, pumps, motors, gearboxes, mobility systems, robots, vehicles, and prospecting/ extracting equipment for ISRU (In-Situ Resource Utilization). Dust impact affects all missions to the lunar surface. It is critical for NASA to better understand how great a threat the dust will play on the lifetime of all these critical assets. This project touches almost all systems for lunar surface exploration/infrastructure, so as such, there are many stakeholders that can benefit from the understanding and advancement of this technology. Successful completion of this project will identify mitigation strategies with potential to improve bearing life in the dirty lunar environment by reducing torque and/or dent depth in contaminated bearings by 15% or more. This project also advances testing techniques and provides performance data for existing bearing seal technologies that is virtually nonexistence from any other source.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Dust Mitigation Development – Dust Tolerant Mechanisms (DMDEV DTM), is a project with the goal of advancing the durability of rotating mechanisms exposed to regolith dust on the lunar surface. For successful sustained human presence on the Moon, hardware exposed to the extreme environment must prevent or tolerate dust intrusion to provide long life with minimal maintenance. The DTM project sought to advance the state of understanding of the technical problem by baselining commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) bearings under exposure to simulated lunar dust to quantify the negative effects on performance. Following the COTS testing, several advanced design configurations of bearings and bearings plus seals were tested to advance the SOA in bearing performance with extreme dust exposure. The knowledge gained will be critical to the development of future hardware for lunar surface operations. The project required development of a new test capability to expose bearings to simulated lunar dust in a representative manner while rotating and collecting performance data. In addition, a new capability to fabricate candidate seals was developed, and a high precision bearing measurement instrument was acquired and commissioned at NASA GRC, enabling the current test plan, and continued future development. Post-testing analysis showed existing technologies to prevent dust infiltration in bearings works effectively to keep bearings free of dust, but at a significant cost of frictional losses (i.e. power loss). Spring-loaded PTFE (trade name: Teflon) seals that have been used in combination with felt seals are especially effective at preventing dust infiltration in bearings. However, the torque required to overcome frictional losses in these seals, specifically spring-loaded PTFE, is an order of magnitude larger than the torque to rotate just a bearing. Other possible sealing configurations tested can be nearly as effective with much lower power loss. Felt seals can be effective by themselves in certain applications. PTFE shields (non-spring-loaded) can be effective, especially when combined with a grease packing barrier. The previous are examples of preventing dust intrusion that can be effective with low power loss. On the other hand, NiTi materials show promise in being tolerant of intrusion where prevention is not possible. Thus, the results of this project indicate that there is a spectrum of possible mitigations to the bearing dust problem depending on the level of cleanliness required and the amount of excess power available in the prime mover of a mechanism. The results will be documented in a forthcoming technical paper to make these results available to the relevant technical community. Further testing should be conducted in a vacuum and cryogenic environment to further advance the technology readiness level, but is currently beyond the scope of this project.
