Project Description

This research aims to record the past experience of automated systems (robots)in the form of generative models that support state predictions into thefuture. We argue that background knowledge can be used to: compensate for missing observations; direct active information gathering; monitor future exposure to risk, avoid actions (and states) that lead to unrecoverable future states; and support a large variety of learning and planning algorithms. Our goal is to create new technologies that respond actively to uncertainty that threatens safety and performance on mission. The research will be conducted by the Laboratory of Perceptual Robotics at the University of Massachusetts Amherst---a leader in the hierarchical skills for autonomous robots, constraint satisfaction in systems that learn by exploration, and the representation of control knowledge. Our group has access to several state of the art bimanual mobile manipulators that we plan to use in an experimental evaluation of learning and planning in this representation in the context of multi-objective activites involving autonomous robot grasping and manipulation with a bimanual mobile manipulator.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The goal of the project is a new straightforward means of representing background control knowledge to support autonomous decision making. As future missions require extended presence at larger and larger distances from Earth, supervision from Earth-bound mission control will incur growing performance penalties due to communication latency. At the same time, this kind of mission will likely require greater logistical, maintenance, and operational support---perhaps with periods of exclusively autonomous control and robotic crew. This project is contributing basic research into appropriate representations that can be used to extend safety and performance guarantees to systems that learn and plan autonomously for extended periods of time in situ. We have developed a control architecture for complex, hierarchical, and autonomous systems. These types of systems will be increasingly important to future NASA missions ranging in scale from autonomous robot systems [Robonaut (JSC), Astrobee (Ames)] to space station ecosystems of people and constituent autonomous systems [Julia Badger (JSC)]. Moreover, this work addresses huge manufacturing, healthcare, and agricultural applications on Earth. Our ESI project focuses on scalable support for autonomy in robot systems and autonomous decision making. These systems require a means of estimating uncertainty in current (and future) states and actively guaranteeing safety and efficiency. Two experimental platforms have been developed for this work, both mobile manipulators. The first is a reduced dimensionality simulation and the second is our 14 degree of freedom uBot, which is comparable in complexity to several kinds of NASA flight system robots. The proposed architecture for our demonstrator platform is presented in Figure 1. Based on state feedback z, the belief distribution over latent states x and the probability that those states were generated by “object” o is computed. The architecture is designed to leverage probabilistic knowledge of objects by using their distinctive transition functions Pr(s’|s, ai) in response to different choices for action ai to differentiate important control contexts. Belief over objects provides a probabilistic model of the range of possible outcome states s’ that might occur when executing action ai from state s given the current level of uncertainty. Control decisions condense belief in the models that could have generated the history of observations and increases confidence in the prediction of future states. We are using a vector of control feedback like this over many such control objectives---called an aspect---to define a control context. Control decisions, therefore, are selected that engage optimal combinations of actions/objectives to meet the specifications of the fully coupled system and to avoid prohibited states. This is the job of the Active Belief Planner (ABP) in Figure 1. The current implementation of the ABP is in the form of an approximate solution to the POMDP that gathers information until confidence in the current control context guarantees future states and excludes actions that would violate system specifications. Supervisory levels of control select the specific combination of objectives to engage sequentially to transform the observable system state into nominal operating conditions in a manner that is consistent with the current latent control context, while it simultaneously optimizes the belief that the system level controller has correctly recovered the control context. The basic research supported by this ESI grant studies how probabilistic models (transition dynamics in a POMDP) can be learned efficiently and then used to determine a distribution over likely outcome states. Over the course of our ESI, we have: 1., 'demonstrated how learning high-probability, task-independent corridors in the state-action space can be exploited at run-time to meet probabilistic safety and performance guarantees; 2. contributed a means of estimating confidence in generative models using a Hough voting/consensus algorithm in the context of our simulated and real robot platforms; and 3. studied learning and exploration strategies for efficiently acquiring models of task-independent transition dynamics and consider the impact of these choices on the run-time performance of autonomous systems. These theoretical motivations and laboratory results are the subject of two recent PhD Dissertation proposals, that will continue beyond the closing date of the grant. Moreover, in addition to autonomous system development for future NASA missions, the contributions of the sponsored research has a broad potential impact in all learning and planning applications for autonomous control, including on-going efforts in autonomous cars, as well as future military, manufacturing, service robotics, and enterprise-level system control.
