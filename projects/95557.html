Heaterless Hollow Cathode Ignition and Operation

NASA TechPort Project ID: 95557
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

The proposed research project aims to study heaterless hollow cathode ignition and develop technologies that extend lifetimes of iodine fueled hollow cathodes. While most modern electric propulsion systems operate using inert gasses, the proposed use of iodine has advantages in its higher storage density and simplified storage requirements. Hall thrusters have been shown to perform well while operating with iodine, however there has not been much success in operating hollow cathodes with iodine for extended periods of time. Implementing both iodine and inert gas feed systems in a spacecraft is undesirable. Therefore, improvements must be made to extend hollow cathode lifetimes before iodine electric propulsion systems can be considered for spaceflight. The proposed research project would experimentally test a broad variety of hollow cathode designs using iodine in order to find the most effective configurations. Heaterless hollow cathodes can be beneficial to electric propulsion systems by reducing complexity and increasing reliability. Currently, heaterless hollow cathode development is suffering from a lack of understanding of the ignition process. The proposed research includes an in-depth study of the ignition process of heaterless hollow cathodes. The ultimate goal of the research would be to develop a heaterless hollow cathode that operates successfully with iodine. To elaborate on what will be done, traditionally configured hollow cathodes will be investigated first. These hollow cathodes include a cathode tube that contains a cylindrical emitter and is terminated with an orifice plate. After configurations like this are investigated, we will investigate cathodes configured in a planar geometry. I have developed cathodes that eliminate the cathode tube by simply placing a disc shaped emitter upstream of a keeper orifice plate. In these cathodes, the emitter is mounted to a thin sheet of refractory metal that has holes that allow for unrestricted propellant flow to pass over the emitter region and out through the keeper orifice. These planar cathodes have performed well when running with argon and xenon, and I believe they will perform well with iodine. The planar geometry allows us to introduce the iodine vapor through a structure that is at a moderate temperature which results in minimized erosion. By thermally isolating the emitter adequately from the planar cathode mount, we will be able to operate the emitter at temperatures that are high enough to prevent iodine from reacting with the emitter material. This technique is similar to the one used in halogen lamps that were developed in the 1970s which showed to have thousands of hours of lifetime.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: A hollow cathode is a critical component in modern plasma thrusters. To operate a hollow cathode, it is necessary to first complete an ignition process in which a thermionic arc discharge is established between the cathode and an anode electrode. Then, during plasma thruster operation, electrons emitted from the hot cathode are used to ionize propellant gas and/or neutralize ions in the exhaust plume. To initiate the ignition process and form a thermionic arc, a conventional hollow cathode utilizes a resistive heating element to preheat the cathode emitter (often to a temperature of 1000 °C or more) prior to ignition. While this approach is effective, the cathode heater introduces volume, mass, cost and complexity. With the advent of plasma-propelled small satellite missions, many of which aim to use low-cost, miniaturized Hall-effect thrusters, the development and integration of cathode heaters has become increasingly burdensome. By forgoing the use of a cathode heater entirely, heaterless hollow cathodes offer a highly attractive solution; however, with no means to preheat the cathode, the ignition process in heaterless hollow cathodes is inherently more troublesome. The aim of this research was to experimentally investigate the ignition process in heaterless hollow cathodes with the goal of devising a heaterless ignition scheme that could feasibly be integrated into a small-satellite propulsion system. To begin, a series of experiments were performed to determine the conditions required to form an arc discharge within a cold hollow cathode. It was quickly determined that, to ignite a heaterless hollow cathode, an elevated propellant flow rate must be provided to the cathode while a relatively large bias voltage is applied between the cathode and an auxiliary anode, also known as the keeper. Further investigation revealed that the diameter of the keeper orifice and the axial distance between the cathode and keeper electrodes has a profound effect on the relationship between the propellant mass flow rate and the bias voltage required for heaterless ignition. Finally, it was found that the most repeatable and reliable ignition conditions were observed at very large propellant flow rates. Given the ease of ignition at high propellant flow rates, this approach was further explored. In the next stage of this research, a propellant feed mechanism was devised that facilitates the heaterless ignition process by providing a relatively large propellant flow rate for a brief duration. The design of this system was driven by the need to minimize cost and complexity to ensure that integration into a small satellite propellant feed system remained feasible. The resulting “fixed-volume release” system provides the propellant flow necessary for heaterless ignition by suddenly releasing a pressurized charge of propellant (initially stored in a fixed volume) into the feed line leading to the hollow cathode. A model of the temporal flow behavior (which was later validated experimentally) showed that, upon the initiation of a propellant release, the flow rate through the hollow cathode quickly rises to a large value before slowly decaying to the nominal value. Heaterless ignition testing showed that the fixed volume release system can be tailored such that an “instant start” heaterless ignition behavior is achieved. In an instant start ignition, a stable arc discharge is formed very quickly in a manner that is highly repeatable and reliable. This behavior is desirable in electric propulsion applications because it allows for a thruster to be switched on with little-to-no readiness time. The final stage of this research involved the investigation of cathode erosion, a problem which has been identified in previous studies of heaterless cathode ignition. It is commonly believed that, to drive a high-current discharge between cold electrodes, a cathodic arc process must be involved. During a cathodic arc, a relatively small volume of cathode material is explosively vaporized and ionized, forming the discharge medium. In some heaterless ignition schemes, cathodic arc activity is involved during the first moments of the ignition process. After some time, sufficient heat is deposited into the cathode to raise its temperature to the point that the cathodic arc can transition into a thermionic arc, after which the hollow cathode is operated normally. It is characteristic for the cathodic arc emission site to move randomly along the cathode surface as a function of time. This process leaves behind a distinct chain of erosion craters (each generally on the scale of 10s of microns in diameter). Because the sacrificial use of cathode material could lead to premature failure or unreliable performance, it was deemed necessary to characterize this erosive activity as a part of this heaterless ignition research campaign. To do so, an experimental apparatus was developed that allowed for a relatively large number of heaterless ignition cycles to be performed using an automated test sequence. This apparatus utilized a fixed-volume release system to provide the temporary elevated propellant flow condition necessary for instant start ignition behavior. After performing several test sequences, (with some samples subjected to several thousand ignition cycles), examination of the cathodes revealed no signs of cathodic arc erosion. To summarize, over the course of this research, it was found that the use of a relatively large propellant mass flow rate can provide the conditions necessary to achieve instant start ignition in a heaterless hollow cathode. Furthermore, it was found that, when using this approach, no signs of cathodic arc activity are observed, and no other forms of ignition-induced cathode erosion are apparent. To facilitate instant start ignition, a fixed-volume release system can be used. This style of propellant feed system is customizable, and the temporal flow behavior can be tailored to accommodate a range of cathode sizes and geometries. The overall concept was demonstrated in a NASA laboratory, and it is likely that the findings from this work will be applied in electric propulsion systems in the near future. Specifically, the concept is presently being considered for use by ExoTerra Resource, Apollo Fusion, Space Exploration Technologies, and the Technion.
