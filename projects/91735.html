Innovations in Understanding and Modeling Cryogenic Propellants for Long-Duration Spaceflight

NASA TechPort Project ID: 91735
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

Space exploration by humans and robots benefits from optimization of many systems. Design of cryogenic rocket systems, typically using liquid oxygen and liquid hydrogen or liquid methane, is crucial to do as well as possible so as to be able to deliver the space vehicle to where the exploration and scientific research can occur. Warming of these chilled propellants shortens mission lifetimes, drives up cost, and, when poorly controlled, prohibits us from even attempting some missions. This research focuses on delivering much-needed improvements to how we model the heat transfer processes, such as warming and the use of chillers, with cryogenic propellants. Unique ground-based experiments along with detailed computer modeling of evaporation, condensation, motion, and heat transfer in the liquid and vapor phases are combined to open up the details of how fast these propellants move heat and mass around the fuel tanks in space by evaporation and condensation. This will permit designers to better optimize spaceflight systems to enable new missions, increase affordability, and reduce risk.

  • Closed Out: Creation of new capabilities in computational modeling of the evaporation and condensation of cryogenic propellants and the validation of these capabilities against liquid hydrogen evaporation experiments were performed. While evaporation and condensation may be slow processes at one point on the liquid-vapor interface in a well-designed cryogenic tank in spaceflight, when large surface areas and long periods of coast in weightlessness are considered, the energy and mass transfer by these phase change processes becomes substantial and very important to the spacecraft design and to enabling the desired mission capabilities. Through a series of investigation of the details, the classical concept of accommodation coefficient is supplemented with a sub-grid model for thin films, resulting in a model that performs well compared with an experiment. Nevertheless, the work also identifies the increased accuracy and precision are needed in experiments to further test such modeling advances.
