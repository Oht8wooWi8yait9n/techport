Control and Modeling of Extensible Continuum Robots

Project Description

The goal of this research is to develop fundamental control theory, dynamic modeling, and control technology for extensible continuum robotic manipulators. These systems often resemble snakes, tentacles, or elephant trunks that are able to bend at any point along their backbone, making modeling and control difficult. Current literature contains partial realizations of these goals but have yet to see advances that allow for extensive application of continuum manipulators. The pursuit of these goals will require the application of linear and nonlinear control techniques, Lagrangian mechanics, state estimation techniques, and system feedback manipulation. Achieving these goals will open the door for reliable control of continuum manipulators and permit Space and exploration applications such as whole-arm grasping, sample manipulation, and crevasse exploration.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Continuum robots are compliant mechanisms capable of bending along their backbone, drawing inspiration from elements of the natural world such as plants, tentacles, and elephant trunks, among others. This compliant structure is useful for applications in exploration, inspection, and manipulation, but also carries with it challenges for deriving models that are useful in the control of more traditional robotic systems, largely due to its theoretically infinite number of bending points along the backbone. The compliant nature also presents a unique problem for human interfacing since the types of motion these robots are capable of do not map well to the motions of our own limbs and bodies. The goal of this research was to improve the interfacing and control of extensible continuum manipulators, including the creation of new mathematical models for improving control and increasing the autonomy of this class of robot through motion planning methods. Our approaches for interfacing included developing interfaces that are kinematically and structurally similar to continuum robots, implementing haptic feedback in a continuum interface, and testing readily available interfaces such as video game controllers. With respect to the modeling and control, we worked to develop approximate dynamic models that capture the core nature of continuum robot dynamics and then applied control methods that address the unknowns and unmodeled nonlinearities that are missing in the approximate models. The primary objective of this research was to enhance the control and interfacing for extensible continuum manipulators. There were multiple components to this overall goal, with the first being the development of intuitive input devices for the teleoperation of continuum robots. Next, going beyond human teleoperation, another goal of the work was to explore multiple automation modalities that enable continuum robots to be deployed and perform tasks without direction from operators. The final goal component of this work was to develop and exploit spatial dynamic models for extensible continuum manipulators accompanied by effective control strategies that support both the teleoperation and automation of continuum robots. These models and control laws function to enable smooth, controlled operation of the manipulators throughout their mission. The approaches used to advance the state-of-the-art in continuum interfacing included developing a haptic interface with impedance-based feedback, establish bilateral teleoperation over long-distances, and create simulated environments in Gazebo physics simulator and MATLAB for validating interface feedback. The automation of continuum robots was promoted by using machine learning to optimize robot search policies for inspection and implementation of kinematic-model-free control for automated motion to desired set-points and trajectories. Continuum robot dynamics were explored by developing a custom Robot Operating System package that enabled physics-based simulations of robot deployment. A middle ground between model complexity and real-time capability was explored by creating approximate spatial dynamic models through Lagrangian dynamics and rigid-body kinematics The research resulted in the culmination of multiple elements that advance the overall state of the art in continuum robot research. Chief among these developments was the first haptic continuum interface, enabling bilateral teleoperation of continuum robots and opening up the ability to investigate and implement long distance teleoperation of these systems. The work also resulted in multiple examples of continuum robots using machine learning to automate designated tasks, increasing their independence when deployed on long term or remote missions. Finally, modeling and simulation tools helped advance the practicality of these robots and their ability to integrate alongside other systems, both currently in use and those yet to be created, to carry out NASA missions.
