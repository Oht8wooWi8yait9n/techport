Visual Positioning System for the Formation Flying of Suborbital Vehicles and Spacecraft

NASA TechPort Project ID: 91658
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

This proposal aims to advance the technology of precision formation flying with the further development of an innovative visual positioning system. This positioning system allows for the precise alignment between multiple spacecraft and precise orientation relative to background stars over large separations. The precision in both relative and absolute position is far greater than that attainable with other current methods and is less reliant on precise timekeeping and ground-based information; allowing for precise positioning well beyond near-Earth distances. The development of such a system has direct application for maintaining the line of sight to external occulters. Using visual information from beacons on the observing telescope and from the target star, our system provides the positional information needed to place the occulter between the telescope and the star. We propose to further develop our working prototype of this system and extend its use to the formation flying of suborbital vehicles and spacecraft. Extensive ground testing will advance our prototype to a level suitable for a suborbital demonstration. A demonstration of centimeter-precision alignment of a Suborbital Reusable Launch Vehicle at 100s of kilometers separation will prove the capabilities of the system and warrant an in-space demonstration. Our positioning system and the technology developed through this proposal has the potential to vastly improve the capabilities of formation flying. An improvement in the technology of precision formation flying of spacecraft is necessary for future success of external occulters and many future space missions.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Advanced From
  • Closed Out: The research conducted under this fellowship was an investigation of formation flying (FF) sensors and technologies with a direct application to enabling technical demonstrations of starshades. Over the course of the fellowship, I designed, built, and tested a number of FF sensors to enable orbital and suborbital starshade missions. One such sensor was demonstrated in a flight campaign on a Vertical Takeoff Vertical Landing (VTVL) rocket and was used to show the viability of VTVL rockets as a starshade testbed. The development of a FF sensor for a solar telescope enabled astronomical observations with a starshade that were some of the first ever taken, and were the most flight-like measurements of a starshade to date. These observations also provided the first visible light photometric measurements of stars in the proximity of Vega. Finally, a diffraction optic numerical code was developed to be used for predicting and tolerancing the on-orbit performance of a future starshade mission. This optical model is currently being validated through field experiments of small scale starshades and was used to conduct a theoretical analysis of a third FF sensor that could be used in the proposed WFIRST starshade mission.
