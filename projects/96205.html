Laser Thompson Scattering Investigation of Plasma Oscillations in Hall-Effect Thrusters

NASA TechPort Project ID: 96205
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

Hall effect thrusters are identified as a leading propulsion system candidate for deep space missions and applications related to human exploration. The NASA In-Space Technology Roadmap identifies maximization of thruster lifetime and prediction of flight performance as central challenges for Hall effect thruster development. Transient plasma oscillations such as the spoke and breathing instabilities are known to have an impact on thruster performance and life. Understanding the source of these oscillations and predicting their occurrence remain some of the significant challenges of Hall effect thruster development. These instability modes appear to originate deep in the ionization region of Hall thrusters, in areas where direct plasma measurements cannot be made without disrupting the oscillation of interest for study. The objective of the proposed research is to use non-invasive Laser Thompson Scattering to study plasma oscillations in Hall effect thrusters. The data shall be used to vet hypotheses about the mechanisms that drive these instabilities. In addition, maturation of the Laser Thompson Scattering technique with application to Hall effect thrusters will enable the diagnostic to be applied as part of the development and qualification activities for flight thrusters. The proposed research supports NASA TABS element 2.2.1 by increasing understanding of plasma oscillations in Hall effect thrusters and the impact on thruster performance and life.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The goal of this research is to investigate plasma oscillations in Hall effect thrusters using non-invasive laser Thomson scattering. Thomson scattering shall be used to measure electron temperature and the electron energy distribution function (EEDF) at the channel exit plane of a thruster. Collection and interpretation of this data will address the research goal through two mechanisms: 1. Measurement of the EEDF at thruster discharge settings where global or local-mode oscillations occur will allow the researcher to examine the relationship between the exit plane EEDF and plasma oscillations in the acceleration region and near-field plume. This investigation will yield insight into the connection between thruster oscillation modes and cross-field transport. The connection with the electron drift instability (EDI) is of significant interest, which is predicted to occur in the near field of the channel exit plane and is directly accessible with Thomson measurements. The researcher is also interested in using EEDF measurements to investigate the formation of local mode oscillations in the channel; there is some evidence from numerical models that EDI may be related to channel oscillations through secondary electron emission (SEE) at the channel wall. Thomson scattering is not presently a time-resolved diagnostic at Hall Thruster plasma densities; therefore experiment designs will be considered that allows for the recovery of time-resolved information using a second diagnostic to mode-lock the laser. 2. Computational modeling of Hall thruster discharges via particle, fluid, kinetic, and hybrid codes is employed to validate numerical models of plasma behavior and enable model-based design and optimization. A Maxwellian EEDF is frequently assumed for the transport coefficients for electrons implemented in such codes. There is evidence from direct measurements with probes and analytical models that the shape of the EEDF is non-Maxwellian. The collection of direct measurements of the EEDF will increase the fidelity of these computational models.
