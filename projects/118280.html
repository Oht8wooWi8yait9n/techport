Development of Novel Electrolytes for High-Energy Lithium Metal Batteries Operating at Ultra-Low Temperatures

NASA TechPort Project ID: 118280
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

High energy density secondary batteries that can withstand harsh temperature environments are vital for the long term mission of NASA and the operation of advanced electronic devices in space. However, the current intercalation-based lithium-ion battery chemistries are approaching the theoretical limit of their energy density and suffer from extremely reduced performance below -20 oC. Both of these limitations arise from typical organic electrolytes, which lack the electrochemical stability to support high voltage Li metal batteries ( > 350 Wh/kg) and typically employ high melting point solvents with poor mass transport properties that exhibit sluggish ion desolvation during battery discharge. We propose to simultaneously solve these limitations by employing a new class of sub-zero electrolytes through a set of innovative design principles, which have demonstrated unprecedented performance metrics in preliminary studies. We intend to further investigate these design principles through fundamental electrochemistry, advanced materials characterization, and theoretical modeling to produce practical lithium metal full cells capable of operating at ultra-low temperatures with high reversibility.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

This research will produce practical lithium metal full cells capable of operating at ultra-low temperatures with high reversibility.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: High energy density secondary batteries that can withstand harsh temperature environments are vital for the operation of advanced electronic devices in space. However, the current state-of-the-art lithium-ion batteries are limited to energy densities 300 Wh kg-1 and typically employ high melting point solvents that exhibit sluggish ion desolvation during battery operation. Furthermore, while many current battery technologies designed for low temperature can be discharged under these conditions, the charging of these devices requires additional battery warming equipment. To support the next generation of high energy density batteries for aerospace applications, we diagnosed the limitations of current state-of-the-art (SOA) electrolytes for Li metal at reduced temperature, and developed a range of improved systems to surpass them. Our work involved both experimental and computational thrusts, which produced substantial improvements in achievable cyclability and power output down to -60°C over the SOA while revealing the scientific underpinnings of said systems. The primary limiting factor of Li batteries at low temperatures has been found to be largely associated with the sluggish desolvation process occurring at the electrode/electrolyte interface. In SOA electrolytes, we found that such reduced temperatures induce tip-driven dendritic growth at the Li metal anode, and subsequent catastrophic failure. To avoid this negative externality, we opted to regulate the solvation environment around the Li+ ion, which fundamentally defines the desolvation barrier. We found that inducing ion-pairing between Li+ and the anion in solution substantially accelerates the desolvation process. These advancements therefore enabled the reversible cycling of Li metal batteries down to -60°C where the previous SOA systems rapidly failed. Afterwards, we conducted multiple extended efforts to determine the root cause of the desolvation advantages of ion-pairing, which revealed distinct contributions from both the anion and applied solvent chemistry. Full utilization of all these insights yielded incrementally improved performance beyond our seminal work, spanning 3 full generations of electrolytes that eventually enabled pilot-scale Li metal pouch cells cycled down to -60°C.
