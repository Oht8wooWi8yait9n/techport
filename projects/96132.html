Novel thermal management and life support technologies for planetary spacesuits

NASA TechPort Project ID: 96132
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

There is a critical need for advanced portable life support systems (PLSS) and thermal management technologies for extravehicular activity (EVA) on the surface of the Moon or Mars. NASA’s Extravehicular Mobility Unit (EMU) is designed for EVA in low Earth orbit and requires the sublimation of consumable water to maintain a comfortable internal temperature. Furthering initial research by Hodgson et al in a design called the Chameleon Suit, this research will advance novel thermoregulation capabilities including thin, flexible, tunable electrochromic radiators integrated into the fabric of a spacesuit or PLSS backpack. This research will also investigate astronaut perspiration using micropores in a mechanical counterpressure (MCP) suit design. This combination of cooling methods will eliminate or drastically reduce the need for consumable water and reduce PLSS mass. This work will require computer simulation of the heat loads in both lunar and Martian EVA scenarios, thermal vacuum testing of several electrochromic device (ECD) and spacesuit layering configurations, and preliminary field testing of these materials on human subjects in analog science EVAs on Earth. Results of this research will provide unique benefits to the development of thermal management technologies for future EVA suits, small spacecraft and satellites, and space habitats.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Future long duration human missions to the Moon and Mars require improvements in the efficiency and reliability of spacesuit life support systems. The goal of this research is to design and evaluate elements of a portable life support system for a mechanical counterpressure (MCP) spacesuit such as the MIT BioSuitTM. MCP suit designs offer the potential to dramatically reduce suit mass and improve astronaut mobility, comfort, and productivity. The first specific contribution is the design, fabrication and test of a prototype breathing compensation bladder for the BioSuitTM. This prototype was manufactured in collaboration with the Dainese D-Air Lab® of Vicenza, Italy and was tested for inflation and pressure-application functionality in the Human Systems Lab at MIT. Results are incomplete but show potential for the design to meet the physiological requirements of an MCP EVA suit. The second contribution is a thermal modeling study of lunar and Martian EVA scenarios to evaluate the thermoregulatory capabilities of different pressure garment and Portable Life Support System (PLSS) configurations. Several garment layups were analyzed in addition to the differences in thermal requirements for female and male astronauts. Results showed that significant work remains to be done in engineering MCP garments that provide adequate insulation while maintaining breathability for heat rejection in the full range of environmental conditions expected. However, the modeling did show that the thermal conditions of both simulated planetary EVAs wearing an MCP suit were survivable, and with suit thermal property modifications could become mission acceptable.
