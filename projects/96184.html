Space Object Shape Characterization Using Light Magnitude Measurements From Multiple Observers

NASA TechPort Project ID: 96184
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

In recent years, there has been a large increase in the amount of orbital debris around Earth. With debris traveling at speeds upwards of 17,000 miles per hour, objects as small as paint flecks can pose a threat to personnel, vehicles, and satellites. For this reason, characterizing and tracking debris is becoming more and more important to maintaining the safety of space-based assets. The orbits of debris depend on the shape, material properties, and other characteristics of the debris through drag and solar radiation pressure. For this reason, it is desirable to determine characteristics of debris objects via observation in order to better predict their orbits. The goal of this research is to investigate the use of light curve data from multiple observers in order to estimate space object geometry. Multiple-model adaptive estimation approaches and neural networks can be used to simulate multiple candidate geometries, and together with light curve data, determine the most likely geometry. Implementing these techniques, adapted to accommodate multiple observers, will help NASA improve mission safety through collision avoidance with increased knowledge of debris locations and orbits.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: SAttitude (or orientation) information is critical to most aerospace applications. It is necessary for standard spacecraft maneuvers such as docking and pointing, and also when accounting for solar radiation pressure and drag when propagating orbits. Conventionally, attitude information is obtained from onboard sensors, such as gyroscopes and star trackers; however, this may not always be possible. In the case of debris, natural satellites, or in the case of loss/lack of communication or sensor failure, these sensors are unavailable or non-existent. In these cases, it is necessary to obtain attitude information externally. While some approaches to external attitude determination already exist (radar, lidar and resolved imagery), these approaches are all limited by resolution and power requirements. The goal of this research has been to achieve external attitude determination using light curve measurements, rather than those conventional measurements. Light curves are potentially an alternative source of attitude information; however, attempts at utilizing them for this purpose have encountered significant challenges. Conventional filtering approaches, such as the multiplicative unscented Kalman filter, require an initial guess at the attitude state, and it has been shown that this guess must be prohibitively accurate to obtain good results when using light curves, limiting the applicability of conventional filtering approaches. This research has identified several phenomena in the light curve model, related to symmetries and the object geometry, that make the static attitude determination problem indeterminate, meaning that it is impossible to identify a unique attitude from the measurements (instead there are a number of ambiguous attitudes). It was also shown that the addition of extra observers, or multi-spectral measurements can resolve these ambiguities. Using this knowledge, a novel multiplicative particle swarm optimization approach (MPSO) was developed that can generate initial attitude guesses with no a priori attitude information, overcoming the initialization challenges that have plagued previous approaches. It was also shown that this MPSO approach could successfully initialize conventional filters, and in the case of high sensor noise, a more sophisticated Gaussian mixture filter could be used to obtain good results, even when the optimization approach began to fail.
