Project Description

With the increase of robotic manipulation tasks (TA4.3), specifically dexterous manipulation tasks (TA4.3.2), more advanced computer vision algorithms will be required for robots to autonomously interact with their environment. Largely, teleoperation has been used up to this point, but this strategy is not effective for extra-planetary robotics. One of the major advantages of robotic manipulation is as a force multiplier, allowing one astronaut to supervise and manage multiple robots or have a robot, such as humanoid Robonaut 2, assist a human astronaut in completing what would be a multi-person task. Without autonomy, most robots require at least one, sometimes multiple, dedicated operators to successfully complete a task. Additionally, long-range teleoperation from a ground control is not viable for dynamic tasks due to the large latency and potential for communication dropouts associated with extraplanetary missions. With teleoperation no longer an option, autonomous manipulation becomes a requirement. To this end, there have been huge advancement in planning and manipulation, but most of these systems require fiducially tagged objects or only work on a small subset of accurately modeled objects. Without an accurate, real-time vision system in the loop to continually reduce uncertainty, these manipulation systems lack the robustness required for actual field work. We are proposing a visual-world model that is consistent with the known properties of our environment, such as static stability and non-intersection, as well as the physics of the sensors. This can be broken down into three major components: the generative model, the physics filter, and the exploration action planner. Tactile and visual data are processed by the generative model to produce candidate object models and poses. These models and poses are tested in the physics simulator for static stability and inter-penetration. The results of these tests are fed back into the generative model to improve the next prediction. Data about regions of high uncertainty are passed to the exploration action planner so uncertainty reducing actions can be recommended.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

This project is developing computer vision algorithms to advance robotic manipulation tasks (TA4.3), specifically dexterous manipulation tasks (TA4.3.2) that will be required for robots to autonomously interact with their environment.

  • Closed Out: Over the extent of this grant, I have developed several state of the art object perception systems, with a focus on uncertainty and self-supervision. Specifically, the direction of my research has been on developing good object representations for robotic manipulation. A good representation can convert a complex motion estimation task to a relatively simple optimization and make learning difficult policies significantly simple. When working with real-world robotic systems, two things are necessary for a flexible and robust object representation. It must be able to, firstly, handle new objects that were not seen at test time, as the real world contains numerous objects that the robot may need to interact with. Secondly, the representation must be able to adapt to the particularities of its current task and environment. This adaptation can mean making the estimator more robust to persistent environmental noise or clutter, or focus on the aspects of the representation which most improve task success. The work described is in furtherance to this goal. In my initial work, we explored the uncertainty inherent in existing representation generation systems. Specifically, we studied uncertainty distributions for existing pose estimation algorithms. In this work, we investigated two methods of learning distribution over an object's orientation. These methods took into account both the inaccuracies in the pose estimation due to dataset biases and occlusion as well as the object symmetries. Our first method regressed the parameters of an isotropic Bingham distribution given the latent representation generated by existing pose estimators. We found that this method well modeled the uni-modal distribution of non-symmetric objects, but was unable to model the multi-modal distribution, commonly found with symmetric objects and high levels of occlusion. Our second method generated a non-parametric histogram distribution over orientation space using a learned feature comparison function. We found that this method was able to model arbitrary distributions for objects with unknown symmetries, accurately modeling both symmetric and non-symmetric objects, without any requirement of symmetry annotation. We further showed that both of these methods can be used to improve the accuracy of existing pose estimators through confidence filtering, allowing the robot to act only on estimates it found to be accurate. https://bokorn.github.io/orientation-distributions/ Later, we explored the use of self-supervised learning with respect to 3D perception. Specifically, we focused on learning to track multiple 3D objects using non-rigid scene flow. Previous state-of-the-art scene flow estimation methods required densely annotated flow vector fields to train scene flow networks with supervised learning or were trained over large synthetic datasets. While good priors are learned in these synthetic environments and the resulting representations transfer to the real world, it has been shown that accuracy improves by nearly 20% when real data from the target environment is used in training. To capture the performance boost of fitting to the target environment without requiring the painstaking annotation of dense flow vectors, we developed a method to train scene flow using two self-supervised losses. The first loss was based on nearest neighbors, which brought the transformed points closer to the target points, and the second loss was based on cycle consistency, which ensured that flow estimates forward and backward in time formed a closed loop. This allowed us to train our method on large unlabeled datasets in our target environment and match the performance of state-of-the-art supervised methods, using no real world annotations. https://just-go-with-the-flow.github.io/ We then returned to 6D object pose, focusing on zero-shot learning and removing the need for annotated data or retraining when estimating the pose of unseen objects and scenes. Specifically we leveraged the inherently zero-shot nature of classical hypothesis generation systems and a learn scoring function that generalizes to objects not seen at training time. Using a classical method to generate a large number of candidate poses, we then rendered each pose into the scene using a sparse rendering technique and computed a validity score for each pose. The highest scoring hypothesis was selected and passed to the downstream task. Using unordered point differences, we limited the ability of our scoring system to overfit to the training objects and scenes. The resulting method accurately localized novel textured and untextured objects in heavily cluttered environments. We demonstrated that, with the help of standard surface reconstruction techniques, we were able to quickly scan a novel object, build a model, and immediately start estimating its pose. Using this rapidly acquired model, we showed that estimated scores for this novel object could be used similarly to our previous uncertainty estimation, allowing us to predict the quality of the pose estimate and perform an object specific grasp using only high fidelity pose estimates. https://bokorn.github.io/zephyr/ We have recently looked towards the use of relative supervision for learned orientations. Relative orientations can be easier to generate than supervision in many cases. Robot kinematics or local registration methods can easily provide this supervision in practice. While one can naively use a rotation based loss between pairs of close rotations, this can fail to converge even in the easiest of cases. We developed a projection based method to supervise sets of rotations using relative supervision that performs better than directly optimizing with respect to the manifold of rotations. We showed that this method could be integrated combined with convolutional networks to learn object orientations. We showed that this method has better theoretic behavior than the standard approach and show empirical evidence that our method converges faster, more often, and to a more accurate end result than state of the art alternatives.
