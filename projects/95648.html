Predictive modeling of chemical and structural failure of porous ablative materials

NASA TechPort Project ID: 95648
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

Post-flight analyses of atmospheric entry vehicles, such as the Mars Science Laboratory (MSL), indicate that current ablation models may be highly conservative, therefore motivating further model development. During the past decade, significant advances in predictive ablation modeling have been made. For example, models are now capable of resolving the thermal protection system (TPS) microstructure, as well as predicting the flow physics and gas-surface chemistry within porous microstructure. However, predictive modeling approaches for the structural response of the TPS are currently missing. Modeling TPS structural response is a considerable challenge, since the structural response and possible failure of the TPS is tightly coupled to both the chemical processes (such as oxidation) occurring on the microstructure and the boundary layer aerothermodynamics (shear stress and heat flux). The goal of the proposed research is to develop a predictive modeling capability that couples thermochemical TPS response with its microscale thermo-structural response. Such a capability would enable precise assessment of TPS microstructure response, including complex processes such as spallation, and could ultimately be used in the design of new TPS materials at the microstructure level, such as new woven TPS currently under development at NASA.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The current state-of-the-art approach to simulating hypersonic flow and material response involves 3D computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations that are coupled to a material response simulation of the thermal protection system (TPS) material. The material response models are typically 1D models, however, there has been recent effort to extend to 2D and 3D models of the in-depth porous material response. Although these 2D/3D models use mesoscale material parameters such as porosity, permeability, and directionality to the microstructure (i.e.carbon fiber) orientation, they operate at the macroscopic scale and do not resolve the material meso- and micro-structure itself. In related research, it is now possible to either scan via tomography, or computationally generate, TPS meso- and micro-structure for use in simulations. These studies have focused mainly on flow through porous material (permeability) and diffusion of reactive species into the porous media (surface vs. volume ablation). There are several aspects missing from the above state-of-the-art approaches. First, the role of convective loading (shear stress and heat flux) from a boundary layer has not been included. Second, the thermo-structural response of the TPS micro-structure, including oxidation and ablation needs to be included. For prediction of TPS failure, it is essential to model both the thermal and mechanical response of these materials including micro- and meso-scale variability of properties within the TPS material. The objectives and accomplishments of this research involve the development of a predictive modeling capability that couples thermochemical TPS response with its microscale thermostructural response. Specific accomplishments include: (i) simulating boundary layer flow over resolved microstructure, (ii) modeling the thermo-chemical response of the microstructure including pitting, (iii) modeling the thermo-structural response of the microstructure, and (iv) developing a macroscopic simulation methodology that can use properties calculated at the microscale so that, eventually, variability of the samples can be accounted for. Specifically, the material response code KATS was updated with a new thermo-chemical-structural coupling framework. The approach to define thermal and structural properties was also adapted to account for stochastic variability. This allows for the implementation of new crack modeling algorithm, and a new spallation algorithm. Some of the properties for FiberForm, a material used for thermal protection, were also updated using synthetic representative volume elements. These new capabilities will enable assessment of TPS microstructure response including certain failure modes and could ultimately be used in material design at the microstructure level, such as new woven TPS currently under development at NASA. The new methods developed will also support reliability estimates for NASA TPS performance on future space missions.
