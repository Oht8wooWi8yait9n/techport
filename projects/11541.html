Autonomous Phase-Space Mapping and Navigation for Spacecraft Operations in Extreme Orbital Environments

NASA TechPort Project ID: 11541
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

The objective of the proposed research is to generate a suite of algorithms for the autonomous navigation of highly nonlinear orbital regimes. These algorithms must fulfill several tasks, beginning with the computationally efficient generation of a map between the spacecrafts available maneuvering options and their corresponding trajectories. This map must then be automatically classified and organized based upon the outcome of each trajectory, such as escape, collision, or safe residence within the system. Subsequently, a planning algorithm must utilize this information to select a maneuver that optimally satisfies the safety requirements and science goals of the mission. Extensive consideration will be given to a variety of numerical integrators, ranging from traditional integrators such as the Bulirsch-Stoer method to energy-conserving symplectic integrators, for the most efficient propagation of individual trajectories. Computational expenses will be further managed by actively rediscretizing the total map of the system as particular regions are discovered to be nearly uniform or finely detailed. To aid in classification, the system map will be described within six-dimensional phase space, where each dimension represents one state variable of the spacecraft. Due to the property of uniqueness, sets of points may be conceptualized as enclosing volumes, which propagate through time as non-intersecting tubes within the phase-space. Once all mapped phase tubes have been classified, nonviable maneuvers will be discarded. Finally, a planning algorithm will select a maneuver to optimally satisfy an objective function derived from science goals, fuel policies, and other mission requirements. The proposed autonomous navigation algorithm would enable breakthrough abilities for science and exploration missions. Long-term orbits could be achieved about planetary satellites such as Europa and Enceladus, which are prime destinations of interest for astrobiological research due to the potential existence of tidally heated subsurface oceans of liquid water. Additionally, close proximity operations, landing, and sampling could be conducted at comets and asteroids. These bodies are desirable targets for the study of the early chemistry of the solar system, likely containing clues about planet formation and the origin of life-enabling organic compounds. Heightened autonomous navigation abilities would even prove useful for various methods of asteroid deflection, such as the dispersion of albedo-altering particles to cause a change in solar radiation pressure. The numerous technological benefits of the proposed research directly support the interests of NASA and the NSTRF program.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: This research investigation concerned mission design – that is, planning trajectories in accordance with science goals – at small bodies (asteroids, comets, and small moons), which are highly desirable science targets in addition to their relevance as potential Earth impactors or as exploitable resources. It is well recognized in recent literature, including NASA technical assessments, that conventional approaches to mission design are poorly suited to these systems due to the chaotic, highly non Keplerian orbital motion that results from their very irregular shapes and the stronger proportion of external forces relative to the gravity of the central body. The state of the art for operating in such systems is to simply avoid the most ambitious (and scientifically rewarding) close-proximity operational regimes and instead remain in more distant regions where averaging theory can be leveraged to identify stable, nearly-periodic trajectories. Exceptions to this paradigm, such as surface sampling operations, have thus far been brief and exhaustively planned endeavors. The NSTRF project centered upon the development of a mission design algorithm that could exploit, rather than avoid, the diverse and unintuitive dynamics near these bodies in order to fulfill science objectives such as observing sets of surface targets with specific close-range viewing geometries. This design capability could enable new types of operations and science returns that simply are not possible under conventional approaches. Furthermore, owing to the high sensitivity and uncertainty of these systems, the algorithm was built to subsume guidance considerations by being both robust and reactive to error in the spacecraft state estimate and the system model. Conclusions of the research effort were highly promising. Operating under realistically-motivated levels of uncertainty, the algorithm progressively constructed mission profiles that fulfilled ambitious science goal specifications while meeting safety constraints. Large-scale analyses revealed the relationship between key design parameters and performance metrics, as well as a great diversity of behavior that suggests a rich design space well-suited for the on-the-fly design approach. Runtimes on consumer-grade hardware indicated that onboard implementation of such an algorithm may be within reach with current or near-future space-rated hardware.
