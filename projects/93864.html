Project Description

For the problem of robotic manipulation, wherein a robotic manipulator interacts with objects or its environment using an end-effector (gripper), there have been attempts to quantify and optimize how good a hold the gripper has on an object being manipulated using grasp quality metrics. Traditionally, grasp quality analysis assumes a rigid gripper and a rigid object. However, technologies currently being investigated by NASA including climbing robots, robotic transport of surface assets, and robotic grappling of free floating objects rely on compliant grippers, and grippers who primarily apply pulling and shear loads. Of particular interest are microspine and gecko adhesive based grippers currently under development. For such grippers, traditional grasp quality analyses do not apply. The first objective of this proposal is to develop a stiffness based approach to analyze grasp quality accompanied by the theoretical background to evaluate its effectiveness. It is common to use multiple grippers in parallel to perform manipulation tasks. In the case of climbing robot locomotion it is necessary, as each limb/gripper must grasp the surface and work together to move along its trajectory. To accommodate uncertainties, it is desirable to find grasp regions, rather than single grasps, in which the grippers can be placed to achieve their tasks. A novel method for finding such regions is to frame the problem as a bipartite graph problem, where graph edges represent grasp qualities. From this basis, finding grasp regions equates to finding bicliques corresponding to regions of desirable surface geometry. The next objective of this proposal is to develop algorithms for finding regions that are practically useful for grasping, by utilizing and expanding on current graphical and manipulation algorithms. Practical implementations of these algorithms must allow for time-efficient computation of grasps for use in robot planning. The objective is to use techniques including probabilistic methods and example-informed searches to efficiently generate grasp regions that meet or exceed a minimum quality threshold (safety factor) in real time. Finally, it is sometimes necessary to perform in-situ re-grasps. For a climbing robot a grasp may begin to slip, or perform worse than expected, and re-grasping of the surface will be necessary. The objective is to use tools such as in-situ pull testing to detect poor grasps, and develop methods for using this information as feedback to effectively execute a re-grasp. Development of this technology directly impacts NASA interests by supplying methods of analyzing and planning for robot manipulation and climbing robot tasks, thereby advancing crucial technologies for robotic space operations and exploration.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The objective of this study was to address grasp analysis for emerging and unconventional manipulator types, including microspine and soft robot manipulators, with the goal of facilitating autonomous planning and operation of dexterous manipulation and climbing in missions of importance to NASA. One major accomplishment under this award is the enhancement and evaluation of a new type of soft, anthropomorphic hand made from expandable foam, along with results showing that this hand demonstrates exceptional robustness and dexterous manipulation capabilities. This line of research made available a new methodology for designing robot hands that has since been built upon by a number of other research groups. A second major accomplishment is the development of a theory of continuous manipulation families. The idea is that robust manipulation should not have to rely on exceptionally precise state estimation and control, but instead should take advantage of built-in compliance and tolerances of a particular task to enable operation over a range of circumstances. Our contributions to the state of the art are to formalize the idea of manipulation families and to provide an algorithm for discovery and modeling of such families through simulation. In development are further contributions to the theory of robust contact placement, which are expected to be published at a later date, but whose initial development benefited greatly from the support of this award.
