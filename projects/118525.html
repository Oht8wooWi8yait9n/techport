Project Description

Advances in robotic technology have opened up an era of tremendous scientific discovery in planetary science within our solar system. While NASA has successfully landed multiple rovers on Mars, these robots require a human operators to monitor the rover’s status, analyze sensor data, and command or even directly teleoperate the robot under significant time delay. To harness the full potential of these robotic systems and enable true human-robot teaming, we propose to develop new computational methods for robots to better intuit the goals and strategies of their human counterparts and to understand when and how to act with more autonomy

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

This project aims to unlock latent scientific yield of rbotic platforms in deep space by enabling machines to understand human goals and act with some autonomy. Additionally, this project will increase robot autonomy in some areas of decision-making can free up astronauts to focus on mission-level objectives, scientific inquiry, and monitoring safety-critical systems and develop novel workload metrics that infer workload through observation rather than intrusive monitoring

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: This research addresses the scenario in which one or more robots is deployed remotely or alongside humans with the primary goal of increasing the robot's ability to act with some autonomy in conducting scientific exploration in space (see graphic below). We assume the robot has an opportunity to train with a human teammate/supervisor in a communication-enabled environment but must act in a novel setting where this training may not be fully prescriptive. Furthermore, this framework assumes the robot has a functioning vision/perception module. The objective of our work is to enable robots to learn from humans how to perform relevant scientific and exploratory tasks, adjust their behavior by understanding the workload of their human counterparts, and explain their decision-making to support human situation awareness (SA), a requisite of effective, safety-critical operations, such as space exploration. Specifically, we answer the following research questions (RQs) RQ1 – How can a robot gain insight humans' goals through observing key perceptual cues and motor control actions so the robot can act with more autonomy? To answer this research question, we developed novel machine learning architectures and human-machine interfaces that enable robots to learn human behavioral models, accounting for how humans are heterogeneous and suboptimal in task performance (Chen et al., CoRL’22; Schrum et al., HRI’22; Chen et al., CoRL’22; Schrum et al., CoRL’22; Jayanthi et al., CoRL’23). This research includes a collaboration with NASA JPL that showed we could reverse engineer the decision-making processes of NASA rover planners in planning paths for the Mars Curiosity Rover without the need for a simulator or quality labels from the human planners – only relying on historical paths executed (Jayanthi et al., CoRL’23). RQ2 – How can a robot infer the workload faced by a human counterpart via observation rather than through physiological monitoring or intrusive subjective measures so as to determine what complementary robot behavior would be helpful? First, we conducted a novel human-subject experiment in a robot teleoperation setup where we systematically varied the human’s exogeneous workload and stress (higher and lower in both dimensions) to develop a response characteristic model. We found that responses to workload and stress are unique, confirming that we should not conflate these factors. Further, we showed the interaction effect between workload, its impact on stress, and the subsequent stress of that stress on performance (Y.T. Sam, MS Thesis ’21). Next, we developed multiple machine learning architectures that can learn models of human behavior when humans are performing tasks suboptimality, due to stress, workload, or otherwise (Chen et al., CoRL’22; Schrum et al., HRI’22; Chen et al., CoRL’22; Schrum et al., CoRL’22). RQ3 – How can a robot concisely give a human teammate updated SA when asking for assistance without relying bandwidth-intensive mechanisms, e.g., video replays? We conducted the first experiment – to the best of our knowledge – differentiating the impacts of explainable Artificial Intelligence (xAI) on situational awareness and performance in collaborative human-robot teaming, grounded in a collabrative building activity in simualtion. We found that xAI can improve human SA but degrades team performance due to possible information overload (Paleja et al., NeurIPS’21). We also studied the impact of a variety of xAI techniques, showing that person-specific ratings of explainability of each method (e.g., decision tree, counterfactual reasoning, feature importance, etc.) is more important that the particular method in predicting performance in mulitple-choice question-answering tasks with robot decision support (Silva et al., IJHCS’22). We also developed new xAI methods (Silva et al. AISTATS’22). These papers have pioneered the field’s understanding of the right way to design and deploy xAI to support human SA and performance
