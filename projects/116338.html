Project Description

Under this ACO project, Blue Origin will collaborate with Glenn Research Center (GRC) and Johnson Space Center (JSC) for the advancement of fuel cell electric power system technology applicable to the Blue Moon lunar lander, advancing fuel cell stack TRL and integration towards actual flight hardware. For Blue Moon, this collaboration leverages capabilities and hardware from NASA centers to rapidly develop a preliminary flight capable stack concept for ground testing, leverages existing work (lessons learned, data, tools), and supports mission and architecture studies. This ACO effort looks to advance previous efforts at NASA centers by focusing on a Blue Origin Primary Fuel Cell Power System for Lunar Landers mission/service and our particular operational requirements and specifications for a fuel cell based electric power system. Separtely from this ACO task, Blue Origin will procure and provide developmental fuel cell stack hardware to enable testing under this ACO.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

This ACO project advances the aerospace fuel cell technology towards space-flight realization at an integrated system level rather than the component level. Integrating a hydrogen/oxygen fuel cell stack into a vehicle architecture clarifies the interface and operational requirements while revealing any required technology development prior to flight.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) released the Announcement of Competitive Opportunity (ACO) public/private partnership (Announcement #80HQTR19SOA01) on 2 October 2018. Under this solicitation, Blue Origin, LLC (Blue) entered into a Space Act Agreement (SAA) with Glenn Research Center at Lewis Field (GRC) and Johnson Space Center (JSC) effective 29 July 2019 to 1) Transfer NASA’s institutional knowledge and experience with primary and regenerative fuel cell systems for space applications to Blue, 2) Provide insight into the Blue Moon lunar lander design during the preliminary design phase, and 3) Independently evaluate the current status of hydrogen-oxygen fuel cells for space applications through analysis, envelope testing, and limited environmental testing. The transfer of knowledge and experience occurred through multiple presentations, documents, meetings, and joint activities. This transfer was sufficiently successful to enable Blue to establish a preliminary internal H2-O2 fuel cell test capability and begin stand-alone system design. This additional testing capability reduces development impediments caused by the limited number of test facilities capable of testing H2-O2 fuel cell stacks at scales relevant to NASA missions. This also fosters commercial development activities without government involvement. Insight activities varied significantly over the course of this Task to avoid either interference with activities under Human Landing Systems (HLS) Appendix E, H and/or N activities or the appearance of impropriety pertaining to those activities. The attempts to keep pace with the HLS requirement changes during the initial HLS formulation phase caused unnecessary expenditure of much of the limited labor hours allocated to the Task. The decision was made to conserve resources by descoping insight work to focus on only the primary fuel cell power system design without including vehicle integration. Blue provided two hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell stack test articles manufactured by Infinity Fuel Cell and Hydrogen, Inc (IFCH) of Windsor, CT for testing at GRC and JSC. These stacks consisted of 36 power producing cells and three auxiliary cells with each cell having an active area of 150 cm2 . Unfortunately, these test articles exhibited design and manufacturing issues that critically limited operational performance and complicated the identified testing. JSC conducted performance, vibration, and shock testing which revealed that the stack performance was not influenced by exposure to the vibration or shock environments. GRC attempted to execute the specified envelope testing but the provided test article did not demonstrate the minimum performance requirements necessary to merit continued testing. This poor performance prompted the fuel cell stack vendor to execute a series of diagnostic activities, generate corrective actions reports, and implement multiple changes to the hardware design, manufacturing processes, and assembly processes. These changes will impact existing NASA contracts awaiting fuel cell stack deliveries.
