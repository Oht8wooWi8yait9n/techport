Passive Radiation Shielding: Integrating Multilayer and Multipurpose Materials into Space Habitat Design

NASA TechPort Project ID: 88568
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio

Project Description

The prospect of long-term human spaceflight beyond low Earth orbit poses a unique set of challenges for space habitat designers. One of those challenges is protecting astronauts from the ionizing radiation in space. On Earth, people are largely protected from that radiation by the atmosphere and the magnetic field. But beyond the natural protection mechanisms of the Earth, the space radiation environment can be dangerous for astronauts. It puts them at risk of several severe health problems, including cancer, central nervous system damage, and cataracts. Although there are several sources of ionizing radiation in space, one of the greatest causes for concern are galactic cosmic rays (GCRs). GCRs are isotropic and low in flux. They are a source of chronic radiation exposure, putting astronaut health at risk gradually over time. GCRs are mostly composed of protons and helium nuclei, but the largest concern for human health comes from their minority heavy ion component. The heavy ions in GCRs can potentially penetrate shielding and human tissue. In the interest of enabling a long-term lunar or Martian mission, the radiation protection systems should be improved to better protect against GCRs. One radiation protection method is passive shielding. A passive radiation shield is a material that is placed between a radiation source and a radiosensitive target, designed to absorb the radiation before it reaches the target. A simple example is a patient wearing a lead apron over his or her vital organs, while being exposed to X-rays at a hospital. In space, passive radiation shielding is more complicated than it sounds, because the variations in particle composition and energy spectra make it difficult to develop a catchall shield. For long-term, human-rated missions, the best material choices for passive radiation shielding tend to be multipurpose, hydrogen-rich, and have a small atomic mass. The purpose of this research is to investigate innovative passive shielding methodologies that are integrated into the design of space habitats. Specifically, this research will evaluate the feasibility of using multilayer materials, water walls, and varied habitat configurations in order to help keep astronaut radiation exposure as low as reasonably achievable. The goal is to improve the shielding efficacy against GCRs, while reducing the amount of parasitic mass required to protect the astronauts. NASA's On-Line Tool for the Assessment of Radiation in Space (OLTARIS) will be used to perform the analyses. This research directly serves to advance the fidelity of topic 6.5.3.2 in the 2015 NASA Technology Roadmap, and ultimately, it aims to advance our ability to explore our solar system.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Managing space radiation exposure is one of the central challenges for enabling long-duration, crewed spaceflight beyond LEO. Space radiation is highly energetic and penetrating, which makes it difficult to attenuate in a mass- and volume-constrained environment like a spacecraft. Prolonged exposure to space radiation can potentially put astronauts at risk of a variety of significant health conditions, including cancer, cardiovascular disease, central nervous system disease, and cataracts. Managing space radiation exposure is a little more involved than simply providing radiation protection, although all things being equal, radiation protection is the preferred way to manage exposure. The difference between the two is that radiation protection is often regarded as a reduction in the dose, dose equivalent, or effective dose, whereas exposure management is simply intended to reduce the severity or likelihood of radiation injury. The latter is an integration and optimization challenge, and it encompasses a variety of intervention strategies that don’t necessarily affect dose quantities, such as those which rely on biochemistry or physiology. The research in this project is divided into two parts: radiation exposure management at the system level, and radiation protection at the technology level. At the system level, this work surveys the trade space for potential ways to manage space radiation exposure, and then it explores a systems engineering framework for using multiple strategies together in order to provide more holistic exposure management. At the technology level, this work takes a closer look at passive radiation shielding by investigating the shielding efficacy and efficiency of layered systems of aluminum and polyethylene, in order to discern big-picture lessons about space habitat design. At the system level, the goal of space radiation exposure management is the short- and long-term preservation of astronaut health. Every mission has specific exposure management requirements that must be met, and in principle, the individual strategies selected to meet those requirements should be the outcome of engineering trade studies. This research reviews nine different exposure management strategies: (1) operational safety, (2) modeling and simulation tools, (3) passive radiation shielding, (4) propulsion improvements, (5) active radiation shielding, (6) personalized risk assessments, (7) pharmacological and nutritional countermeasures, (8) synthetic torpor, and (9) synthetic biology. Additionally, this research explores using a systems engineering framework to design a radiation exposure management subsystem as a combination of those strategies – effectively treating radiation exposure management as a system of systems. The outcomes of this system-level research are: (1) a contemporary review of nine different exposure management strategies, and (2) an exploration of a potential framework for the future of space radiation exposure management. At the technology level, passive radiation shielding is an important part of managing space radiation exposure, and passive shielding is noteworthy for two specific reasons. First, it’s easy to implement. Passive shielding is simple, reliable, and it has previous spaceflight heritage, which makes it attractive to include on missions for which radiation exposure management is a requirement, but not necessarily an engineering design-driver. And second, passive shielding will inevitably be provided by every material onboard a spacecraft. Space radiation is isotropic and highly penetrating, which means that all of the materials between the astronauts and the spacecraft exterior, including the hull, payload racks, and stowage, will inevitably influence the radiation field on the inside of the spacecraft. To that end, NASA has an explicit interest in using hydrogen-rich logistics to help provide passive shielding without having to add parasitic shielding mass. This research investigates the simulated shielding efficacy and efficiency of layered systems of aluminum and polyethylene, which are intended to represent a spacecraft’s structure and logistics, respectively. All of the radiation transport analyses are conducted using NASA’s On-Line Tool for the Assessment of Radiation In Space (OLTARIS), which is an online engineering design tool built and maintained by NASA Langley Research Center. The purpose of these analyses is to create a series of sensitivity plots that illustrate how small changes in the shielding configuration will affect the effective dose behind that shielding. That information is valuable for space habitat designers, because it offers some insight into how habitats could potentially be designed and organized in order to help keep astronauts safe from radiation injury. The outcomes of this technology-level research are: (1) a series of sensitivity plots that illustrate the simulated shielding efficacy and efficiency of layered systems of aluminum and polyethylene, and (2) an explicit list of habitat design lessons which result from that analysis.
