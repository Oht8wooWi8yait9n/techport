Project Description

This project is to develop a Tandem Ion mass Spectrometer that is capable of operating on the moon in anticipation of future lunar lander mission. This work includes using GEANT3 radiation codes to simulate the response of microchannel plates (MCPs) and ultra-thin carbon foils (CFs) to energetic electrons from 0.7 MeV to 27 MeV. As well as using the electron accelerators at the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) in Gaithersburg, MD to directly subject the detector to energetic electrons.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

This project is to develop a Tandem Ion mass Spectrometer that is capable of operating on the moon and, potentially, other bodies in the solar system in anticipation of future missions.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The starting objective was the development of the Tandem Ion Mass Spectrometer (TIMS) for the Simplex-3 opportunity, which was postponed in 2020. Instead, we initiated development of the Ion Velocity Mass Spectrometer (IVMS), which is essentially the front-end of TIMS with some differences. We accomplished the ray tracing design of the IVMS, which allowed us to fine tune its Circular Wien Filter (CWF) to broaden the energy range down to 30 eV for protons and double the instrument sensitivity. In addition, we were able to add steering lenses or deflection plates to the design. We also performed environmental testing at WFF, which allowed us to increase the IVMS TRL to ~ 5. The IVMS can be used as a 3D solar wind monitor. It can also be used to measure the solar wind composition. In addition, this versatile instrument can be used to investigate the magnetospheres of planets and/or their moons. One potential application involves deploying the IVMS on lunar landers and/or lunar orbiters to take measurements of the solar wind interaction with the lunar surface. This IRAD development work has matured the technology to the point where it was able to be infused into the GIRAFF sounding rocket mission (Robert Michell PI), which will study the aurora, with a projected launch date of ~ March 2023. This should allow us to achieve TRL ~ 6. The purpose of the Goddard Space Flight Center’s Internal Research and Development (IRAD) program is to support new technology development and to address scientific challenges. Each year, Principal Investigators (PIs) submit IRAD proposals and compete for funding for their development projects. Goddard’s IRAD program supports eight Lines of Business: Astrophysics; Communications and Navigation; Cross-Cutting Technology and Capabilities; Earth Science; Heliophysics; Planetary Science; Science Small Satellites Technology; and Suborbital Platforms and Range Services. Task progress is evaluated twice a year at the Mid-term IRAD review and the end of the year. When the funding period has ended, the PIs compete again for IRAD funding or seek new sources of development and research funding, or agree to external partnerships and collaborations. In some cases, when the development work has reached the appropriate Technology Readiness Level (TRL) level, the product is integrated into an actual NASA mission or used to support other government agencies. The technology may also be licensed out to the industry. The completion of a project does not necessarily indicate that the development work has stopped. The work could potentially continue in the future as a follow-on IRAD; or be used in collaboration or partnership with Academia, Industry, and other Government Agencies.
