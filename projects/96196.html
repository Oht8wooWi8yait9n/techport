Micro-scale modeling of ablative thermal protection systems during atmospheric entry

NASA TechPort Project ID: 96196
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

When spacecraft enter an atmosphere, they require the use of a thermal protection system (TPS) to mitigate the harsh aerothermal environment. NASA relies heavily on computational modeling to design and size TPS for planetary missions. However, post-flight analysis of NASA missions often reveals discrepancies between predicted and actual behavior, and highlights the need for improved first-principles modeling. High-fidelity modeling of ablation poses significant challenges due to the multi-physics and multi-scale nature of the phenomena, and the difficulty in conducting experiments representative of atmospheric entry conditions. One area of potential improvement is in better understanding the micro-scale behavior and decomposition of heat shield materials, and upscaling the micro-scale behavior to macro-scale modeling. In this proposed work, a multi-physics computational framework will be developed to study the micro-scale decomposition behavior of ablative thermal protection systems. The proposed research will include modeling high-temperature flow though porous media using Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) methods, given the non-continuum conditions that exist during an atmospheric entry. Within the DSMC framework, oxidation at the fiber scale, including pitting dynamics (the non-uniform way in which carbon fibers oxidize), as well as solid and radiative heat transfer, will be simulated. Oxidation modeling will be coupled with mechanical failure modeling of the carbon fibers, including thermal stresses and fluid-structure interactions. The computational framework will be used to model the thermochemical and mechanical erosion that occurs when a spacecraft enters an atmosphere, including those with atmospheric dust, such as Mars. The proposed work aims to improve NASA’s understanding of micro-scale material response of TPS, and use that understanding to improve macro-scale modeling and help NASA prepare for the entry systems challenges of future missions. In particular, this work hopes address fundamental questions in the mechanical erosion of heat shield materials, a critical source of uncertainty in atmospheric modeling.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: A common problem in materials modeling is the existence of multiple length scales that, due to computing limitations, cannot be modeled simultaneously. In the example of modeling NASA heat shield materials, system-scale simulations are needed in order to predict overall material performance. At this scale, however, the microstructure of the material cannot be simulated directly. It turns out that for many materials science applications, including heat shield materials, understanding microscale characteristics of a material are critical to predicting overall material behavior. To capture these microscale effects, system-level models rely on the use of “effective” models that serve as closure terms for the macroscale equations. Estimating these closure terms is, in practice, achieved through a combination of experimental characterization techniques and by direct numerical simulation at the microscale. Advancements in X-ray microtomography, a 3D imaging technique capable of resolving a materials microstructure at sub-micron resolution, has allowed for these direct numerical simulations to be performed on high fidelity representations of a materials microstructure. Before starting my PhD, I began the development of the Porous Microstructure Analysis (PuMA) software at the NASA Ames Research Center to serve as a framework for large-scale direct numerical simulation of material properties based on X-ray microtomography. From past work, as well as ongoing collaborations with the other developers of the PuMA software, PuMA can estimate many material properties including porosity, surface area, mean pore diameter, thermal conductivity, elasticity, electrical conductivity, permeability, and tortuosity
