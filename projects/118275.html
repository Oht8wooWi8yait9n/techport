Project Description

The Rotating Magnetic Field (RMF) Thruster is a device inspired by Field Reversed Configuration (FRC) research in both the fusion and electric propulsion community. It uses a pulsed rotating magnetic field to drive plasma currents to produce thrust. Because of its pulsed nature, the resulting high-power thruster would be highly throttleable due to the ability to adjust duty cycle. The RMF current drive mechanism also requires relatively low currents relative to other pulsed inductive thrusters, potentially offering dramatically increased lifetime. Additionally, it would be ideal for in-situ resource utilization (ISRU) applications because the confined nature of the plasmoid and lack of plasma-wetted electrodes ensures minimal erosion of the device even with propellants such as water. However, relatively little work has been done to study the mechanisms behind accelerating the plasma, and measurements of thruster characteristics (thrust, specific impulse, etc.) have never been performed. We propose to study this promising new technology by adding onto the existing analysis of the acceleration of FRC plasmoids, and by testing a real device to achieve the first real measurements of its performance. The results of the theoretical and experimental portions will then be used to begin the optimization of the RMF thruster. This project allows for research which fits in with the expertise and long-term plans already in place in Prof. Jorns lab, while maintaining that the main research effort is in search of a next-generation ISRU-compatible thruster.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

The resulting high- power thruster would be ideal for in-situ resource utilization (ISRU) applications because the conned nature of the plasmoid ensures there is no contact with the walls of the device.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The purpose of this fellowship was to measure and optimize the Rotating Magnetic Field (RMF) thruster. After initial literature review, the RMFv1 thruster was built. This thruster was based primarily on the ELF thruster, designed and tested by MSNW, LLC. The RMFv1 consisted of an aluminum body with the RMF antennas mounted on the inside of the aluminum cone. A LaB6 hollow cathode was used as the seed ionization source. The RMF supply produced the oscilating currents required to generate the rotating field via capacitor discharge into the antennas, causing LC resonance at the RMF frequency, approximately 50 kHz. While we were able to install this thruster on a thrust stand, negligible thrust was measured. High speed video showed some ionization occurring, but not a significant amount.Lessons learned from this experience, along with additional analytical analysis which suggested several major shortcomings of the device, we designed and constructed the RMFv2. Rather than an aluminum body, which had the effect of screening out the rotating magnetic field and thus reducing antenna/plasma coupling, we used a non-conductive G10 design with the cone wall consisting of sheet mica cut into the cone shape. A new power supply was designed and built in conjunction with Eagle Harbor Technologies as part of an SBIR grant, which operates using a different architecture. Each antenna is paired with a tuning capacitor bank to form an LC resonator, and pulses are sent at the resonant frequency to produce high currents. Through this method, we achieve voltages across the antennas nearly 10 times that feeding the power supply rack. Tuning capacitor sizes were selected to operate at approximately 400 kHz, an order of magnitude higher than previous.
