Planning the Motion of a Robotic Assistant for Space Applications

NASA TechPort Project ID: 91521
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

Revolutions in computing hardware and software have brought about an age where robots and people will be able to peacefully and safely co-exist in the same environment. Robotic assistants have become increasingly relevant for tasks that are mundane or dangerous, and a driving force for the development of this technology are applications intended for space missions like those specified in NASA strategic roadmap STR04. Unfortunately, there is a vast deficit in automation when it comes to robots and people working the same environment, either separately or cooperatively. Today robots are either used in isolation, or are completely controlled by a skilled human operator. This work will develop the foundations for an automated robotic assistant, furthering the state-of-the-art in autonomous robotic motion planning and planning under uncertainty, i.e., noisy sensors or imperfect actuation. In this proposal, it is argued that planning must consider not only the short-term goal of moving to a particular point in space, but leave the system in a state where a higher-level task can be accomplished. It is also argued that uncertainty must be considered at the very early stages of planning. Current state-of-the-art planners are only able to handle planning under uncertainty for simplified systems. However, advances in planning algorithms have recently shown that realistic modeling of many kinds of uncertainty, known as planning in the belief space, can be performed in real-time for even complex robots. By incorporating planning considerations for both short and long-term goals alongside realistic modeling and planning for uncertainty, one major step toward the goal of an autonomous robotic assistant can be realized. Several techniques will be investigated ranging from reasoning over only a critical subset of the belief space while accounting for uncertainty, to modern Bayesian methods, which attempt to reason over the entire belief space and construct an optimal policy; the policy instructs the robot at every point in its environment. An automated robotic assistant would directly benefit NASA's goals in robotics, particularly in the sub-fields of autonomy, planning, and uncertainty. The proposed work is envisioned primarily for systems such as Robonaut 2. However, the work will be conducted in a principled and general way that will allow its application to other systems as well. Moreover, robotic autonomy has broad implications not only for NASA's interests, but also the potential for societal impacts as well. For example, an autonomous system which performs routine maintenance aboard the International Space Station could translate into a household robot which is able to assist in caring for elderly or disabled persons.

  • Closed Out: The complexity of modern robots often obviates the use of reactive control schemes or direct human operation to maneuver the system to a particular state. Motion planning is an inherently deliberative process, and the generation of motion plans that are both robust to local phenomena and achieve the desired outcome requires reasoning over both the short and long-term consequences of each action the system takes. As robots become increasingly productive and capable, either by increasing the degrees-of-freedom (DoFs) of the system or by modeling dynamical and external effects on the physical system, however, the difficulty of quickly computing a high-quality motion plan becomes insurmountable with current state-of-the-art methods. The size and dimensionality of a high-DoF search space is simply unwieldy for optimal algorithms, and the redundancy of feasible solution paths often results in obviously sub-optimal motions. Moreover, modeling dynamical effects such as acceleration or torque can easily slow the search by one or more orders of magnitude, even for a system with relatively few DoFs; reasoning over stochasticity in the system’s dynamics adds yet another bit of complexity in the motion planning problem. This work has utilized the following mission statement while investigating some of the difficult challenges in state-of-the-art motion planning discussed above: The goal of this work is to develop, analyze, and deploy generalized algorithmic motion planning techniques for robots with a broad spectrum of capabilities and various levels of uncertainty. New methods should be amenable to systems with complex physical constraints and reason effectively over challenging cost landscapes. During this fellowship grant, research into the motion planning problem was conducted on two fronts: efficient motion planning for high-DoF kinematic manipulators and fast task-planning for dynamical systems under uncertainty
