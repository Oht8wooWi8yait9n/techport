Project Description

Microwave Kinetic Inductance Detectors are highlighted in NASA’s 2015-2045 roadmap as a game-changing technology for improving remote sensing instrument and sensor capabilities (TABS 8.1.13). MKIDs are poised to overtake the more established CCD detector technology because they are easy to multiplex into large arrays, can deliver a time resolution of 2 microseconds, and are capable of single photon counting without read noise or dark counts with energy resolution across the ultraviolet, visible, and infrared (UVOIR) spectrum. Currently, MKID arrays require large, complex, power-intensive readout electronics which present a major obstacle to MKID deployment in space. I propose to develop a new MKID readout system which is faster, lighter, smaller, and over an order of magnitude more power-efficient than the current technology. Specifically, I plan to decrease the readout system weight by 85% and volume by 85% all while using 1/20th the power. I plan to accomplish this by incorporating the newly released Xilinx programmable computing chip: the Zynq UltraScale+ RFSoC. With this hardware change, I will need to redesign and test the firmware using MATLAB/Simulink and Vivado Design Suite and write a new python control library. This project directly addresses NASA’s goal to reduce the volume, mass, and power of on-board electronics and supports advanced computing and data architectures in remote sensing instruments (8.1.2). It also supports Technology Area 11.4: Information Processing.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Microwave Kinetic Inductance Detectors (MKIDs) are capable of single-photon-counting with energy resolution across the UV, Visible, and Ultraviolet spectrum and microsecond timing precision. This unique combination of abilities coupled with the fact MKIDs can be multiplexed into large arrays makes these detectors a primary technology target for integration into future NASA instruments. This work focused on scaling MKID readout technology to advance MKID technological readiness for large ground-based instrumentation and space flight. Two major advances of this work are a new superconducting, flexible, coaxial (FLAX) ribbon cable that supplies cryogenic readout signals to the detectors and an RFSoC-based digital readout system (MKIDGen3) capable of reading out 2,000 detectors in real-time. The FLAX cable is an ultra-low loss, crosstalk, and heatload, cabling solution which improved the readout performance of existing 20 kilopixel arrays. In the MEC instrument, FLAX installation increased the number of usable pixels by 20% and in the XKID instrument, the average detector energy resolution increased by a factor of 4. FLAX is currently under commercial development by Maybell Quantum Industries and paves the way for future larger arrays with tighter heat budget. MKIDGen3 is capable of reading out twice as many detectors as the previous system with ⅕ the power and a fraction of the weight and volume of the previous system. MKIDGen3 combines new RFSoC hardware with a python-backed embedded software framework and ZMQ-based server architecture to facilitate simplified array setup. These improvements have increased the feasibility of future MKID instruments and allow us to conduct novel studies in the lab to improve detector performance.
