Leveraging Protonic Solid-Oxide Electrochemical Cells for Process Intensification of H2 Recycling and In-Situ Resource Production

NASA TechPort Project ID: 186809
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

To accelerate NASA's goals in Surface Sustainability and Logistics and to prepare for future Mars missions, the development of in-situ resource utilization (ISRU) technologies is essential for both long-haul flights and planetary settlements. These technologies can facilitate the in-situ production of return-launch fuel, crew consumables, and feedstocks for manufacturing. A critical component in this effort is the Sabatier reactor, which converts atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) and H2 produced in-situ into methane (CH4) and water (H2O). The lack of robust technologies to manage the mixed gaseous outlet stream (H2, CH4) generated by the Sabatier reactor poses a significant obstacle to efficient resource recycling, such as H2 recirculation. NASA has identified a need for new versatile technologies to (1) isolate pure H2 from mixed H2/CH4 streams (Shortfall 1583) and/or (2) enable reactive capture of CH4 to generate additional H2 and plastic precursors in the form of C2 and C3 molecules like ethylene and propylene (Shortfall 1534). Herein, I propose a new approach leveraging protonic ceramic solid-oxide electrolysis cells (H-SOECs) for hydrogen and methane separation and conversion to critical manufacturing or fuel feedstocks. The proposed technology could significantly improve Sabatier reactor performance via H2 recycling, purify CH4 for liquid fuel production, and generate material feedstocks for manufacturing . Beyond the Sabatier reactor, this technology can be used to upgrade alternative mixed gas streams such as pyrolysis waste gas (Shortfall 589). H-SOECs offer several advantages for hydrogenation/dehydrogenation chemistries, including high protonic conductivity at a wide range of operating temperatures. H-SOECs work by abstracting protons (H+) and electrons (e) at the anode electrode surface, where protons are transported across the solid-oxide electrolyte and generate products at the cathode, such as the evolution of molecular H2 gas or the hydrogenation of oxygen into potable water. This chemistry can be harnessed in a compact, modular electrochemical device with the potential to achieve both selective H2 separation and controlled CH4 dehydrogenation into H2 and C2+ manufacturing feedstocks. With this approach, our proposed H-SOECs can operate in two modes in the presence of a mixed H2/CH4 stream: (1) Separation mode (400-600C) and (2) Reactive capture (RC) mode (>600C). Switching between these two modes is achieved by using temperature and applied potential as levers for control over reactivity and product selectivity. To assess the performance of H-SOEC technology, well-defined and robust electrocatalysts must be developed. Size-modulated Pt catalysts dispersed on perovskite supports will be synthesized and characterized via physicochemical analysis (Aim 1). The performance of Pt/perovskite electrodes will be evaluated through electrochemical testing and H-SOEC cell testing. In-situ vibrational spectroscopy will be utilized to gain a mechanistic understanding of electrochemical H2 and CH4 oxidation (Aim 2, 3). Electrodes will be tested at accelerated aging conditions and re-analyzed to evaluate stability and resistance to coking and sintering (Aim 2, 3). Finally, H-SOEC cells will be tested with various gas feedstocks to identify the optimal applications for this technology (Aim 4). The knowledge gained from this project will contribute to the implementation of H-SOECs for the Space Mission Technology Directorate's goals in Surface Sustainability and Logistics.
