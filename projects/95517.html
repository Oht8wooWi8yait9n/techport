Methane to carbon nanotubes for clean energy and advanced materials

Project Description

This proposal focuses on improving growth processes for carbon nanotubes (CNTs) as an enabling technology for lightweight CNT yarn conductors and CNT-based composites. Its primary objective is refining the understanding of the dynamics of CNT growth via the floating catalyst chemical vapor deposition (FCCVD) method. This will serve as a means to improve process efficiency and the quality of produced CNTs. Specifically, improvements to CNT aspect ratio (length/diameter) and crystallinity are desired. Its secondary objective is to investigate the use of the FCCVD process for the simultaneous production of CNTs and hydrogen from methane. This could provide a scalable alternative to steam-methane reforming for the production of hydrogen for use as rocket propellant. Improving the quality of available CNT is an important step in realizing the property milestones laid out in TA10.2.3.1 and TA10.1.1.1. The properties of these and other macroscale applications of CNTs scale with the quality of the CNTs used in their manufacturing. Current CNT production methods remain poorly characterized from an engineering standpoint. This has resulted in an inability to commercially produce CNTs that allow macroscale applications to reach any significant fraction of the properties of individual CNTs. The FCCVD process is one of the most promising methods to grow CNTs in a manner suitable for industrial production, but more studies of internal processes like reaction kinetics and heat and mass transport phenomena are needed. The FCCVD process can be operated with a variety of hydrocarbon feedstocks, including methane. The result is solid carbon in the form of CNTs and hydrogen gas, which may easily be collected and used as fuel. This hydrogen has, up until recently, been largely ignored as byproduct of the reaction. Creating a process optimized for the simultaneous production of CNTs and hydrogen will build upon and go beyond the studies proposed in the pursuit of the primary goal. A variety of techniques will be used to study internal processes of FCCVD and their effect on the produced CNTs. The high temperatures used in FCCVD will necessitate a combination of in situ condition measurements and sample collection, model experimental systems, and finite element modeling to build a holistic understanding of the process internals. Produced CNTs will be characterized using the spectroscopic, microscopic and thermogravimetric techniques common in the study of CNTs. These include Raman spectroscopy, transmission electron microscopy, and thermogravimetric analysis.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The overall objective of this NASA Space Technology Fellowship (NSTRF) funded research project was to improve the efficiency and controllability of the floating catalyst chemical vapor deposition (FCCVD) synthesis of high quality (i.e., long, highly crystalline, and low impurity) carbon nanotubes (CNTs). CNTs serve as an enabling technology for next generation, light weight conductors, fibers, and structural composites of wide utility in aerospace applications. Costs for commercial high quality CNTs range from $1,000/kg to $10,000/kg. This makes macroscale applications that take advantage of their uniquely high mechanical strength and electrical conductivity prohibitively expensive in most scenarios. The basics of the FCCVD process used to produce most commercial CNTs are straightforward. A gas flow of a carbon containing species (e.g. a light hydrocarbon or organic molecule) and an aerosol of transition metal catalyst nanoparticles (either preformed, or generated in-situ from a reactive precursor species) are introduced into an empty, tubular furnace at temperatures ranging from 800°C to 1350 °C. Other components such as inert gasses, hydrogen, or chalcogen containing species are typically added to control aspects such as residence time and soot formation, or to accelerate the growth of the CNTs. Despite this apparent simplicity of the process, the interconnectedness of aerosol dynamics, reaction kinetics, and transport phenomena makes the system difficult to comprehensively study and model. The primary approach of this research was primarily to examine the sub-processes of the overall FCCVD system to gain actionable engineering knowledge that can be used to improve the production efficiency and quality of the carbon nanotubes. Specifically, it has focused on understanding the genesis and evolution of a catalyst aerosol from an organometallic precursor, the role of un-catalyzed, homogeneous reaction on CNT growth, and the influence of heat and momentum transport in the overall reactor. The harsh environment presented by FCCVD reactors complicates in-situ measurements of many key parameters. Thus, the research used a hybrid approach relying on both physically informed modeling of aspects of the process as well as CNT growth experiments to test hypotheses and inform new modeling directions. Knowledge gained from the synthesis of these two approaches was then leveraged to develop new process recipes and reactor designs. Over the course of the NSTRF funding this approach resulted in design knowledge that was applied to achieve significant improvements in process productivity and key quality metrics. CNT production rate has improved 50-fold, from ~0.01 g/hr to ~0.5 g/hr. This was achieved simultaneously with improving the quality of CNTs: The fraction of CNTs in the product increased from ~20% to ~95% and CNT lengths increased from ~2 µm to ~20 µm while maintaining high crystallinity. Conversion of the carbon feedstock to CNTs has also improved from <1% to ~10%. Overall these values place us on competitive ground with the more expensive commercial providers, both in terms of product quality and process efficiency.
