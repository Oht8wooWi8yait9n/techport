Project Description

The electrospray thruster array is a potentially revolutionizing technology that could fill the current capability gap that exists in propulsion for small spacecraft. This device in theory can offer unprecedentedly high efficiency and specific impulse at the limited power levels (< 10 W) available on small payloads. These promising advantages have motivated a number of recent experimental efforts both domestically and abroad to develop this technology. The need to further evaluate and refine these proposed concepts for electrospray arrays in turn has given rise to a growing demand for more sophisticated engineering design tools. In particular, computational models are critical to help address major challenges in the fabrication and lifetime qualification of these microscale devices. Our effort builds on the framework of an existing numerical tool, the Electrospray Propulsion Engineering Toolkit (ESPET) developed by Spectral Sciences Inc, in several novel ways. By leveraging a combination of physics-based modeling, data-driven regression, and rigorous uncertainty quantification, we propose to build a numerical capability that will allow users to input their proposed thruster geometry and generate predictions for performance, lifetime, and potential failure modes. The goal is that this toolkit will be a key enabler for ultimately transitioning electrospray thruster arrays to flight.

This toolkit has the potential to be a key enabler for ultimately transitioning electrospray thruster arrays to flight.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The electrospray thruster array (ETA)—alternatively called micro-electrospray propulsion (MEP)—is considered by NASA to be a key enabling electric propulsion (EP) technology for smallspacecraft. Of the top technical priorities listed in the 2015 NASA In-Space Technology roadmap, ETAs ranked as the highest form of small spacecraft propulsion and the 5th highest technology overall. While the promise of ETA technology has motivated several parallel engineering efforts to build and prototype new thrusters, modeling and simulation (M&S) is an equally important tool for establishing the flight readiness of ETAs. It can evaluate the probability of failure modes, identify issues with the ground to flight transition, and assess the risks of spacecraft-plume interactions. This latter concern is particularly pressing for ETAs as they can operate on nontraditional, more reactive metal propellant. The advantages afforded by M&S make it almost impossible to visualize a clear path to flight for ETAs without this numerical capability. Developing M&S tools to establish the capability of ETAs is thus of the highest relevance for this technology. The major technical challenge in developing M&S of ETAs stems from inherent uncertainties in the operation and implementation of these devices. These include uncertainty about how to model the underlying physical processes, e.g. how the emitted current depends on flow rate, as well as uncertainty in the fabrication, e.g. variability in performance due to manufacturing tolerance. Faced with these inherent uncertainties, we proposed to develop a probabilistic approach to modeling ETAs. Our goal was to incorporate self-consistently both modeling and design uncertainties in our M&S to generate mission-relevant conclusions such as “this design has a 90% chance of satisfying requirements for key performance metrics (e.g. lifetime, plume divergence, and efficiency) given our known sources of uncertainty.” In practice, sampling is the major computational challenge with the implementation of a probabilistic approach to modeling. Model uncertainty often can be represented as a range of possible values for the emitter geometry and the model parameters. Thousands to millions of model runs over this range of values are required to generate median predictions and to assess confidence in the model predictions subject to uncertainty. The time required to run high fidelity models with this sampling-based approach can quickly become prohibitive. With this end in mind, we baselined the reduced fidelity Electrospray Propulsion Engineering Toolkit (ESPET) developed by Spectral Sciences, Inc. This numerical tool incorporates all known state of the art scaling laws for the performance of common ETA substrates, geometries, and propellants. These laws in turn are analytical expressions or simple ODEs that can be evaluated rapidly. This is wellsuited for our probabilistic approach. Under this effort, we expanded ESPET’s capabilities in three ways for the purpose of generating probabilistic predictions for performance. (1) ESPET was missing models for some key processes such as divergence that can impact ETA operation. We therefore developed new scaling laws to represent these processes. (2) It was necessary to rigorously quantify the uncertainty in the ESPET models. To this end, we combined Bayesian parameter estimation with experimental datasets to quantify uncertainty both in the pre-existing models in ESPET as well as the new models we developed under (1); and (3) We applied rigorous sampling techniques to assess how model and design-based uncertainty impacted model predictions. In Year 1 of this effort, we focused on building the algorithms necessary for accomplishing the proposed improvements to ESPET. We used a pressure-fed capillary array as a case study to focus these efforts. In Year 2 of this work, we pivoted to identifying M&S-based strategies for optimizing the design of arrays with porous-based emitters. This change was motivated by discussions with collaborators at NASA and members of the wider field who indicated that porousbased emitters are one of the most promising methods to scale ETAs in size (> 1000 emitters) while maintaining competitive thrust densities and performance. To this end, we incorporated our modeling framework developed in Year 1 into a new optimization scheme for numerically exploring the design space of porous emitters. Our target was to identify ETA configurations capable of achieving long life and competitive performance (> 1000 s, > 25% efficiency, and thrust densities > 20 N/m2). In Year 3 of this effort, we focused on improving the fidelity and robustness of our optimization scheme. To improve model fidelity, we developed two refined sub-models for ESPET: a model for divergence angle and a model for electric field at the tip. For this latter module, we applied a uniquely data driven approach in which we employed neural networks to build a surrogate for the results from a more sophisticated electrostatic simulation. To improve our abilities to assess model-based uncertainty, we developed an algorithm for performing model inference from experimental data from an array of emitters rather than just a single emitter. This was a crucial improvement for refining the semi-empirical models in ESPET as most experimental test articles are multiplexed. In parallel with the development of sub-models and performing inference, we built an algorithm for identifying ETA designs that were “robust” to 99% confidence. Allowing for model and design-based uncertainty, we were 99% confident that these designs would produce the targeted performance requirements. In the final year of this effort, which was supported under a no cost extension, we continued to develop new submodels for the ETA and to refine the robust optimization algorithm. To this end, we proposed and validated against experimental data a new scaling law for multi-site emission from porous emitters. We also applied a combination of models to regress data from an Air Force designed array. We subsequently showed that we could match key trends for the performance of this array, and significantly, we found that the behavior of the array could not be explained unless we considered manufacturing uncertainty in the model prediction. As a last step in this effort, we have proposed a design-build cycle for identifying robust electrospray arrays. Here, we will use our algorithm to identify the most promising design for testing, build this array, measure it, and use the measurement to update our models. Our goal is to complete three design cycles before the 2023 AIAA SciTech.
