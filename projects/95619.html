Development of conical off-plane gratings for future X-ray spectrometers

NASA TechPort Project ID: 95619
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

In a traditional X-ray spectrometer, there are four optical elements: a two-part Wolter-I (paraboloid-hyperboloid) X-ray telescope, a grating array, and a detector array at the focal plane. Even with the latest X-ray grating technologies, current X-ray grating spectrometers suffer major throughput losses at the grating array due to unavoidable geometric and reflectivity throughput losses. This proposal seeks support to develop a novel, low-TRL X-ray optics technology which could eliminate this throughput loss by combining the secondary (hyperboloid) mirror of the X-ray telescope and the off-plane grating into a single optical element. To develop this technology, we will need to understand the conical grating geometry and how grating features map to the conical surface. In addition, we will need to determine the best interface between current telescope and grating processes so that we can fabricate the gratings onto the conical surface at the optimal time which does not disturb mirror fabrication efforts. And finally, we must develop nanofabrication methods to manufacture the grating features onto the conical surface. If these challenges can be overcome, conical off-plane gratings have the potential to significantly increase the performance of future NASA X-ray spectrometer missions, vastly increasing the amount of science which could be performed.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The goal of this research was to further the development of X-ray reflection gratings operated in the extreme off-plane mount by implementing a novel soft X-ray spectrometer design which combines the secondary of the two-part X-ray optic and the X-ray reflection grating into a single optical element. This proposed design would reduce the number of elements in the spectrometer by one, thus increasing the throughput of the spectrometer by a factor of 1-4x over a traditional three-element spectrometer. To realize this technology, two paths were concurrently pursued: (1) the fabrication of full-sized reflection gratings onto the secondary surface of X-ray optics with diffraction efficiency and spectral resolution rivaling that of the current state-of-the-art X-ray gratings and (2) the development of alignment techniques to construct curved diffractive X-ray optic (CDXO) modules from individual CDXO elements for integration into space-based missions. The first path proved to be a challenging undertaking throughout this fellowship award. While the theoretical benefits of this new technology were determined and methods to measure the quality of these to-be-manufactured curved gratings were developed, a reflection grating on the curved secondary surface of the X-ray optic was ultimately not realized. However, significant development came from the second of these development paths, where a new reflection grating alignment methodology was developed. This new alignment methodology leverages state-of-the-art nanofabrication methods to manufacture wedged grating substrates to precise tolerances, enabling the gratings themselves to act as an alignment constraint throughout the alignment process. This, in turn, allows future aligned reflection grating modules to be assembled quicker, more accurately, and with less total weight than the current state-of-the-art techniques. In conclusion, while the overarching goal of a two-element spectrometer was unfortunately not realized, this fellowship did indeed further the X-ray reflection grating technology by developing new baseline manufacturing and alignment methods for future observatories implementing this technology.
