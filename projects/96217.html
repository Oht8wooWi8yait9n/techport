An Evaluation of the Influence of Residual Stress on Additively Manufactured Materials through Fatigue Crack Growth Analysis

NASA TechPort Project ID: 96217
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

Mechanical properties of materials that control service life and device capabilities depend on internal structures and stresses established during manufacturing. Additive manufacturing, an advanced method of manufacturing, can fundamentally change the way components are built in space technologies by allowing lightweight complex geometries to be produced on demand. It also offers the exceptional opportunity to fabricate reliable structures and engineering components in remote locations where resupply or repair may not be possible. While additive manufacturing offers significant technological advancements supporting innovative design, it also introduces unique internal structures and stresses, thereby affecting the mechanical properties. My research focuses on evaluating the effects of these internal structures and stresses in additively manufactured materials through fatigue crack growth analysis, providing insight into the variability, reproducibility, and reliability of the resultant material properties. My research will focus on residual stress, the inherent internal stresses present in AM materials in the absence of external loads, as well as the distinctive directional microstructures that develop during the deposition process. In this way, I will contribute to the establishment of processing structure properties relationships, which are necessary for scientific advancement in additive manufacturing technology and its subsequent transition to NASA programs.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Additive manufacturing (AM), an advanced method of manufacturing, offers significant technological advancements supporting innovative design and can fundamentally change the way components are built in space technologies by allowing lightweight complex geometries to be produced on demand directly from 3D computer models and addresses the need for structural innovation to extend NASA’s vision of exploration into deep space. While AM has the potential to impact NASA missions, it still faces technical challenges such as reliability and affordability. This work addressed the reliability of AM materials for space applications through an evaluation of their fatigue behavior. High thermal gradients imposed during the AM process often results in significant residual stress and unique microstructures that influence the mechanical properties that control service life and performance. Using different measurement techniques, the influence of residual stress on fatigue crack growth behavior in AM materials such as directed energy deposited (DED) and powder bed fused (PBF) 304L stainless steel from Sandia National laboratories was evaluated. Values of the residual stress intensity factor (Kres) were quantified and compared in fatigue specimens oriented for crack growth parallel and perpendicular to the AM build direction (vertical (V) and horizontal (H), respectively). Fatigue behavior was examined through fatigue crack growth tests performed at constant and decreasing alternating stress intensity factor (ΔK). The constant ΔK method ensures that the applied stress at the crack tip is adjusted to maintain a constant driving force as the crack grows, allowing crack growth rates to be measured with high confidence and effects of microstructure and residual stress to be evaluated. The decreasing ΔK method advances the understanding of fatigue crack growth behavior by examining near threshold crack growth at low values of ΔK that are typical of service applications. This research has furthered the knowledge of the effects of processing on mechanical properties of AM materials by using fatigue crack growth experiments to evaluate their microstructure and their residual stress variations. Residual stress profiles from slitting measurements show the reproducibility of each process and confirm that the DED material has smaller magnitudes of residual stress as compared to PBF. Measured fatigue crack growth rates reflected the differences in process-induced residual stress in the two materials. Tensile residual stress acting at the crack tip enhanced crack growth and led to faster fatigue crack growth rates in the threshold regime of PBF 304L than the DED 304L. The verified crack compliance method provided an alternative method of measuring residual stress effects without the need to sacrifice material, allowing fatigue crack growth data and residual stress intensity data to be collected in the same test, where the residual stress intensity factor represents the contribution of residual stress on the driving force for crack growth. In this manner, fatigue crack growth rates for all specimens of DED and PBF material could be corrected for residual stress effects. Intrinsic fatigue performance was compared and found to not only be similar to each other, but also similar to the effective fatigue performance of wrought material. Since both materials have anisotropic microstructures, it was concluded that residual stress has a significant impact on fatigue crack growth rates while the unique AM microstructures do not. The results of this work provided insight to the processing-structure-properties relationships necessary for the scientific advancement of AM technology.
