Project Description

A high thrust-high specific impulse propulsion system is needed for safe, fast, and reliable manned exploration of the solar system, and it is required to reach interstellar targets in any sort of appreciable timescale. Most fusion systems could provide this, but most fusion systems suffer from numerous technical challenges, the a primary one being efficient conversion to directed jet power for thrust. Augmentation of the SPFMax (Smoothed Particle Fluid with Maxwell equation solver) modeling software, as outlined in the Scope of Work, to model the magnetic nozzle subsystem of the PuFF (Pulsed Fission-Fusion) architecture, will directly address this challenge. Verification of the code with known analytical solutions and experimental results also serves to address this challenge. In conclusion, full development and test of the PuFF system will provide a high thrust-high specific impulse propulsion system, opening up an entire new class of interplanetary and interstellar missions to meet NASA's goals.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: A medium thrust-high specific impulse propulsion system is needed for safe, fast, and reliable crewed exploration of the solar system, and it is required to reach interstellar targets in any sort of appreciable timescale. Most pulsed fusion systems could provide this, but such systems suffer from numerous technical challenges, a primary one being efficient conversion to directed jet power into thrust, and a secondary one being generation of sufficient power to run the system. The objective of this work is to address both these challenges by putting forth design principles of both the power-generation system and a magnetic nozzle system (to efficiently convert jet power into thrust). We definitively succeed in the former, discovering two non-dimensional parameters to characterize power generation system design: the ratio of initial plasma energy to initial pickup coil energy, and the ratio of initial pickup coil inductance to primary-side transformer inductance. We find that the first ratio should be 0.01, and the second ratio should be 1 for ideal system performance. We also find that for a system to provide 1.6 MJ of energy, its mass is 40 metric tons. Regarding the latter, we discover that two effects should be balanced for a magnetic nozzle design that maximizes performance: plasma deflection and magnetic drag. For a prototypical pulsed fission-fusion plasma (with an initial plasma energy of 1.6 GJ), and for an axial nozzle design with 32 struts, each 5 m in length, the two effects will be balanced at a per-strut current of 7.5 MA. Additionally, as part of this work, we developed a fully 3D plasma simulation software that is two orders of magnitude more computationally efficient than the state of the art (called SPFMax); it allows for many magnetic nozzle designs to be evaluated quickly and simultaneously. SPFMax shows good agreement with experiments and the state of the art so far; continued verification is on-going. Once SPFMax is fully verified and one determines the mass of the magnetic nozzle subsystem, one can determine how to integrate these power-generating magnetic nozzles into pulsed fusion propulsion systems, realizing the goal of a safe, fast, and reliable propulsion system that can reach Mars in 3 months and Neptune in 4 years.
