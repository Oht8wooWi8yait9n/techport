Ultrasensitive Paper-based Biosensors for In-flight Quantification of Key Biomarkers in Saliva

NASA TechPort Project ID: 95494
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

Critical to the success of NASA missions is sustaining the health and performance of astronauts, both during and after the mission. Fundamental to maintaining health is the timely evaluation of each astronaut, by which treatment can be given when needed. However, many of the common clinical laboratory techniques used on Earth can not be used in space, meaning many important health biomarkers cannot be analyzed during missions. In my graduate work, I am developing hyperstable protein nanoscaffolds that can be used to assemble and process gold nanoparticles to make advanced plasmonic biosensors. I intend to use these advanced sensors to create a simple, paper device that can quantify key biomarkers in-flight. This device will be compatible with microgravity, small cargo limits, and already used equipment on board, making it a robust analytical platform. During development, this device will be functionalized to assay levels of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), a key regulator of bone and muscle health that has also been linked to cancer development. To minimize sample volumes and maximize collection convenience, IGF-1 levels will be measured from saliva. To create this device, I will first use a hyperstable protein and advanced protein tools to create rigid protein nanostructures to which gold nanoparticles can be attached. I will then enclose these nanoparticles in a hollow, porous gold shell. Because the close spatial assembly of multiple nanoparticles and the encasing of nanoparticles with gold shells each provide significant improvements to plasmonic capabilities, I anticipate that a combination of the two will compound those enhancements and create sensors with high enough sensitivity to quantify important biomarkers in trace concentrations. Finally, I will functionalize these nanostructures to bind to IGF-1 and adhere them to small strips of filter paper that can then be used in analyzing biological samples. These advanced sensors will not only provide a powerful solution to a key challenge facing NASA and long-duration voyages of the future but also act as an analytical tool that can be used an a wide array of terrestrial applications as well.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Although real-time monitoring of key hormones, metabolites, and other biomarkers in astronauts during spaceflight is vital to the future of NASA missions, the clinical tools used terrestrially to measure those biochemicals are generally incompatible with the limitations of extraterrestrial travel (e.g., functional in microgravity, small cargo mass and volume, low demands on astronaut time and technical expertise, etc). To create analytical devices compatible with spaceflight, we worked to further develop the versatile platform of paper-based plasmonic biosensors. Building upon past successes in improving plasmonic biosensor performance, we hypothesized that assembling and processing gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) on a defined nanostructure would compound the benefits of two previously demonstrated approaches to improving plasmonic sensors. Such sensors could then be tailored to create simple and portable devices for quantifying hormones, cytokines, and other important biomarkers of immune health in minimally invasive saliva samples. At the onset of the project, we proposed to complete the following aims to develop our space-ready sensor devices: 1) create thermostable protein nanostructures that are tunable yet dimensionally defined, 2) assemble and process AuNPs on our protein nanostructures to create advanced plasmonic sensors, and 3) functionalize those sensors to bind and therefore quantify immune health markers in a spaceflight compatible device. For aim 1, we identified the computationally designed three-helix bundle protein (3HB) as an excellent protein building block for molecular structures due to its rigid rod shape and unparalleled stability. We also identified split intein protein domains as protein tools that we could use to ligate multiple copies of this protein into simple two-dimensional structures. For aim 2, we planned to leverage strategically placed cysteine residues to assemble AuNPs in different arrangements on those structures and then enclose those AuNPs in a hollow gold shell. Because the close spatial assembly of multiple AuNPs and the encasing of nanoparticles with gold shells each provide significant improvements to plasmonic capabilities, we anticipated that a combination of the two would compound those enhancements and create sensors with high enough sensitivity to quantify important biomarkers in trace concentrations. For aim 3, we proposed to decorate our improved plasmonic structures with proteins that specifically bound to cortisol or protein cytokines. This binding would result in a concentration-dependent change in the wavelength of light absorbed by these structures, which could be detected using a simple spectrophotometer. These functionalized and validated plasmonic sensors would then be deposited on small strips of filter paper, creating a device that could rapidly and easily quantify immune health biomarkers from astronaut fluids.
