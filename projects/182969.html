Recycling and Reuse of 3D Printed Plant Growth Hardware for Sanitization and Crop Growth During NASA Missions

NASA TechPort Project ID: 182969
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

This project aimed to explore the ability to recycle end-of-life 3D printed components and inedible plant biomass generated during space plant experimentation or crop production. The end goal of this process was to produce new, clean parts thereby reducing the need for mission resupply. Materials were ground and fed through a compounding mixer alongside Polylactic Acid (PLA), a thermoplastic commonly used in 3D printing, to produce the initial generation of simulated recycled material. The study performed testing on components printed with this simulated recycled material to identify a limit for plant material addition as well as the number of times a material can be recycled while maintaining its efficacy. Following printing of each generation, excess printed material was ground to produce the feedstock for the next generation of printing. To measure changes in efficacy, printed coupons from each generation of printed and recycled materials were gathered and tested in the KSC Mechanical and Environmental Testing Laboratory (METL). Both the tensile strength across generations of material and the effect of the added plant biomass compared to recycled, pure traditional printing material were determined. Additionally, microbiological tests were performed to evaluate the viability of 3D printing processes as a sanitization step for recycled materials. A variety of microbiological organisms were tested using printing as well as exposure to dry heat as sanitization steps to determine necessary treatments to maintain cleanliness of parts and equipment.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

The overall anticipated benefit of this project was to provide a baseline data set for evaluation of capabilities of 3D printed materials to be recycled and utilized for production of parts and components. The data gathered can be used to inform future work in terms of material selection, material life expectancy, mass requirements, and necessary treatment steps for recycling. Results and lessons learned from this study also lay the groundwork for future studies involving the utilization and further analysis of recycled materials.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: With KSC IRTD funding provided in 2023, this project examined the capabilities of 3D printing of recycled materials including the addition of inedible plant biomass to simulate what is likely to be a by-product of long-term space crop production. Dried alfalfa hay pellets were used to simulate inedible plant biomass, such as the stems and leaves of bean plants in long duration crop production scenarios. Hay was originally tested, but the labor required to grind this was prohibitive, so pelleted hay was utilized instead. Pellets were ground to a fine particle size before being mixed with PLA to produce a simulated first-generation recycled material. Initially, the plan was to recycle materials into filament spools, but it was quickly identified that this process had the potential to introduce an extra layer of material attrition as each cycle of recycling for spooling and printing, with the resultant spool, would lead to two stages of wear being applied to the material. Also, the filament recycling/extrusion process technology was less mature than indicated by the manufacturer, and filament uniformity was an issue. Instead, materials were printed using a pellet-fed printer (Advanced Regolith Ground Operations (ARGO) test bed in the Granular Mechanics and Regolith Operations (GMRO) Laboratory at KSC) and recycled by grinding printed commodities with the ground pieces serving as the pellets for the next generation of printing. During each phase of printing, a set of Type III materials, dog-bone test articles were printed for mechanical testing in accordance with ASTM D638. This testing revealed a marked decline in material strength following the first recycling process, with slight to moderate decreases during subsequent recycling steps using pure PLA coupons. Interestingly, test articles printed with compounded materials, including biomass, appeared to maintain their efficacy during the first two generations of recycling and printing with a decrease being noted following the third recycling process. Both varieties of test articles were recycled and reprinted to produce five generations of test materials, and these appeared to still be viable for manufacturing following printing of the final generation. Further testing would be required to determine the true limit of material viability of both materials. Microbiological evaluation included printing PLA filament inoculated with a bacterial spore suspension to test the efficacy of the printing process as a means of cleaning recycled materials to print clean parts. Test coupons printed from this inoculated material were not completely able to eliminate the inoculated bacteria, so it was determined that pre-treatment steps would be necessary. The first pre-treatment step tested was dry heat exposure (80 °C) of materials. This is a dual-purpose step, as the process of 3D printing also requires minimal moisture content in printing materials to avoid the production of air bubbles during printing which can disrupt the flow of material and lead to defects in printed commodities. This heat treatment continued the comparison of compounded and pure materials with the treatment step serving as an effective tool for lowering surface microbial loads in both instances. Pure materials required longer exposure to achieve similar levels of cleanliness but were still brought to a level of cleanliness that would be determined safe for use. Following the testing performed in this study, recycling of 3D printed materials has been evaluated as a concept. This should be elevated from the initial TRL of 2 to a final TRL of 4 with a strong indication that it can be further evaluated for eventual use in flight missions or studies.
