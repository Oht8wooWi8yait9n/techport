Development of Secondary Lithium-Sulfur Batteries for Low Temperature Environments

NASA TechPort Project ID: 94015
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

The next generation of space suits, landers, rovers, and spacecraft will require secondary batteries capable of storing larger amounts of energy. As missions venture further and further out from the sun, these batteries will also need to be capable of withstanding increasingly colder temperatures. Lithium-sulfur batteries have been proposed as a successor to traditional lithium-ion batteries due to their high theoretical capacity and energy density. This large energy density could be incredibly beneficial in launches to space from Earth, where minimizing payload mass is essential. However, these batteries will need to be capable of performing in low temperature environments before their use is adopted on space missions to the outer solar system. I propose to research and develop the low temperature performance of lithium-sulfur batteries. I aim to perform this project in three phases. First, I will perform a component by component study on commonly used Li-S battery materials, where I examine the kinetics of the lithium-metal anode, carbon-sulfur composite cathode, and electrolyte (dimethyl ether and 1,3-dioxolane) at low temperatures. I intend to substantiate these studies with surface and morphology studies via the assistance of scanning electron microscopy, transmission electron microscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, and other related techniques. I will then use the information collected in these studies to evaluate, optimize, and develop novel electrode and electrolyte components for low temperatures. I would be interested in developing entirely new protected lithium-metal or alloy anodes, novel sulfur cathode architectures fabricated with electronically and ionically conductive species, and optimized electrolyte compositions for low temperatures. The final step of this project is to construct laboratory scale battery cells that have large capacity, high coulombic efficiency, and long cycle life at temperatures in the range of -60ËšC to 0 ËšC. This project addresses the Energy Storage section of the Power and Energy Storage technology roadmap (TA03). The following tab elements are specifically addressed: TA03.2.1.3, as well as TA03.2.1.1 and TA03.2.1.2.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Electrochemical energy storage technologies with high energy density and will be instrumental in advancing the next generation of space suits, landers, rovers, and spacecraft, which hold the key to further advancing exploration and discovery of our solar system and beyond. For the last few decades, lithium-ion batteries employing insertion compound cathodes and graphite anodes have become commonplace in portable electronics and devices, and have been used on several NASA missions. As the technology advances and applications continue to require higher and higher specific energy, the energy density of secondary batteries needs to similarly increase. Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have been proposed as a possible successor to traditional lithium-ion batteries due to their high theoretical specific energy, which is almost an order of magnitude higher than the state-of-the-art lithium-ion batteries. A critical consideration during deployment of energy storage technologies for space applications is the ability to perform well at low-temperature conditions. Such conditions can present severe kinetic hurdles towards operation, curtailing capacity attainment in environments of interest. The aim of this project is to understand and enhance the performance of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries at performance-critical low-temperature conditions. Li-S batteries face a number of key challenges even at room-temperature conditions arising from the tendency of the sulfur cathode material to dissolve into the liquid organic electrolyte during cycling. While this dissolution of active material can lead to rapid capacity decay, it also promotes favorable discharge kinetics in solution and is intrinsic to the manner by which Li-S batteries operate. Low-temperature optimization in conventional lithium-ion batteries often entails targeted development of the electrolyte, though that approach does not neatly translate to Li-S batteries. Given the solution-mediated complexity intrinsic to Li-S batteries, the optimization of Li-S batteries for low-temperature conditions requires the consideration of altogether new mechanisms and insights in order to optimize the technology for low-temperature conditions. Thus, this project has been conducted in a multi-stage manner, as follows: 1. Fundamental study on the lithium-sulfur battery chemistry via a combination of electrochemical and kinetic studies in order to investigate how the system responds to changes in operating temperature and identification of major kinetic barriers 2. Development of novel cell components that are engineered with the intent of overcoming obstacles discovered in Phase 1. 3. Development of lithium-sulfur cells built primarily from electrode materials and electrolytes optimized and tested in Phase 2. Cells will be tested in both coin cell and pouch cell formats at low-temperature conditions, such that the technologies developed can be evaluated under realistic conditions. Initial studies in this fellowship focused on fundamentally elucidating the underlying kinetic hurdles during Li-S discharge and charge. This work significantly expanded upon the current understanding of the Li-S chemistry, with entirely new and previously unreported mechanisms identified. In particular, this work identified the tendency of intermediate lithium polysulfides to cluster and aggregate in solution at low-temperature conditions, which curtails the diffusion-based behavior upon which the system relies on. The discovery of this clustering mechanism serves as a key accomplishment during this fellowship and provides the framework for significantly accelerated low-temperature development. From there, the work in this fellowship focused on engineering development, including the evaluation of novel electrolyte salts and additives containing the strongly binding trifluoroacetate anionic group. These compounds curtailed the deleterious clustering behavior identified, leading to enhanced performance in Li-S coin and pouch cells at temperatures of interest. The advances in scientific understanding and engineering development conducted throughout this fellowship project have been disseminated to the scientific community through a number of publications (6 first-author publications). The work performed here sets the stage for rapidly increased development and deployment in the near future.
