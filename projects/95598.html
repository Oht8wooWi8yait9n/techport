Optical Technology for Exoplanet Characterization

NASA TechPort Project ID: 95598
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

The goal of this research is to advance optical technology for exoplanet direct imaging space telescopes. This includes building an optical model of the Deformable Mirror (DeMi) CubeSat payload and validating the model through lab testing. The DeMi payload will advance adaptive optics technology by characterizing a Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems (MEMS) deformable mirror’s performance in the space environment, and this research will prepare the DeMi team to understand the results of on-orbit testing. This project will provide valuable on-orbit data to demonstrate a key technology for future exoplanet imaging missions. After this in-flight demonstration of key technology for exoplanet coronography, this research will focus on science instrument technology development, specifically spectrograph design and analysis. We will investigate how new optical and photonic technologies can be applied to save size, weight, and power for these systems. Atmospheric models for extrasolar giant planets will be developed and used to understand the requirements for spectrographs for atmospheric characterization and assess the science return of the proposed instruments.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

This technology will enable advances in the discovery and characterization of earth-like exoplanets.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The goal of this research is to advance optical technology for exoplanet characterization with space telescopes. Specifically, this grant supported the on-orbit characterization of a MEMS deformable mirror and the development of integrated photonic technology mid-wave infrared wavelength range. Microelectromechanical Systems (MEMS) deformable mirrors (DMs) are a key technology option for achieving the extremely precise wavefront control required to directly image exoplanets with coronagraph instruments on space telescopes. MEMS DMs are a promising technology for this application because of their compact size and low power requirements. The Deformable Mirror Demonstration Mission (DeMi) CubeSat optical payload was designed to demonstrate this technology in space for the first time. The payload was aligned, integrated, and tested into a 6U CubeSat in 2018-2019 and was launched into space in 2020. On orbit data from July 2020-March 2022 successfully demonstrated the DM operating in the space environment and performing wavefront control with a Shack Hartmann Wavefront Sensor, reducing the RMS wavefront error from ~500 nm to <150 nm RMS. The second project supported by this grant was MWIR integrated photonics development. Integrated photonics has the potential to drastically miniaturize optical devices by manufacturing nano-scale waveguides and optical devices on integrated chips instead of with bulk optical elements. Miniaturizing optical instruments with photonic technology could provide size, weight, and power advantages and improved robustness for space telescope instruments. However, incorporating integrated photonic devices into space missions is challenging because the field is relatively new and most technology development is focused on devices that operate in the telecommunications wavelength bands. This research developed integrated photonics technology using Germanium (Ge) deposition to demonstrate devices that can operate in the mid-wave infrared (2-5 um, MWIR) wavelength range. The MWIR wavelength range covers atmospheric absorption features that can be used to study greenhouse gas abundances or detect gases in exoplanet atmospheres. This grant supported waveguide and passive component design and demonstrations in this wavelength range. The waveguide loss meets the state of the art performance for the 4-5 um wavelength range with air cladding (0.5-2.5 dB/cm). We demonstrated the first example (to the best of our knowledge) of Germanium waveguides with top cladding in this wavelength range, reaching losses of 4-6 dB/cm with epitaxial silicon top cladding. This work included a study of different top cladding material options for MWIR integrated photonics, which important because top cladding increases the device’s robustness to the surrounding environment and also reduces the complexity of fabricating active components such as phase shifters. Waveguides, narrow-bandwidth filters, input tapers, bends, and waveguide conversion tapers have all been demonstrated in the lab. Additional components such as adiabatic couplers, grating couplers, thermal phase shifters, and integrated PN-diode modulators have been designed for upcoming fabrication runs.
