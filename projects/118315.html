Project Description

A new methodology is presented for simulating ﬂow environments produced during supersonic retropropulsion (SRP), the premier deceleration strategy by NASA to land larger payloads on Mars. SRP is a propulsive decent strategy that is executed by thrusting rocket engines during vehicle descent to decelerate the vehicle as it approaches the surface. The advancement of SRP, though, is contingent upon the accuracy of SRP simulation methods, since there is limited relevant SRP wind-tunnel test data or Mars flight data that could be used for design. Thus, SRP must be developed with the assistance of accurate simulation tools so that future vehicles and their thermal protection systems can be optimally designed for the Martian environment.

Existing simulation methods are unable to capture the complex flow environments produced during SRP and are unable to accurately discern dynamic and thermal loads over the full vehicle body. This work will address these inaccuracies by developing a critically needed simulation tool that will combine large-eddy simulation (LES) with higher-order methods, a novel wall-model formulation that accurately accounts for thermal effects in the boundary layer (WMLES), and embedded boundary methods (EBM) with adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) so as to reduce the computational cost. This proposed work will therefore yield a new simulation tool that can accurately resolve all aerothermodynamic loads to the structure, including proper prediction of flow attachment and reattachment locations, pressures, and temperatures. This will allow for optimized designs of the vehicle’s external geometry, control surfaces and systems, and thermal protective system. Furthermore, the success of this work will also extend to advancing simulation tools for other high-speed, high-enthalpy, multi-physics ﬂow environments which can be found in several NASA air and space ﬂight initiatives, including hypersonics.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Supersonic retropropulsion (SRP) is the only descent technology that meets future NASA crewed Martian mission requirements for landing high mass payloads at high ballistic coefficients. Importantly, the development of SRP to technology readiness is heavily reliant upon computational fluid dynamic (CFD) simulations due to minimal experimental and flight test data of SRP maneuvers. This computational dependence is further exacerbated by the parametric design process. Therefore, the goals of this work are two-fold. First, is to create the ideal simulation methodology for SRP by answering the question: what level of fidelity is necessary for simulating SRP to accurately predict vehicle loads? The second goal of this work is to perform uncertainty quantification on these SRP simulations and therefore answer the question: how confident are we in these simulation results? Accomplishments that align with the first raised research question include the development of a start-up simulation protocol to perform wall-modeled large eddy simulation (WMLES) SRP simulations at high Mach numbers, high angles of attach, and with multiple nozzle configurations. These results were published in “Large-eddy Simulation of Supersonic Retropropulsion Test at NASA Langley Unitary Plan Wind Tunnel” at AIAA SciTech 2022. Importantly, this work was one of the first to report results for LES of an SRP configuration. To address discrepancies between the WMLES and published experimental test results for vehicle loads on the nose, especially at nozzle exits, a detailed study on wall functions was conducted and presented in “Examination of Wall Functions for Simulating Powered Descent” at the Interplanetary Probe Workshop (IPPW) 2022. Then, the newly developed Modified Reichardt Wall Function was successfully applied to simulating SRP in “Discernment of Wall Functions and Turbulence Statistics for Common Supersonic Retropropulsion Configurations” at AIAA SciTech 2023, and more accurately predicted vehicle loads than the commonly used Reichardt Wall Function. This publication also included a detailed study on the generated turbulence, something that previous Reynolds Averaged Navier Stokes (RANS) based simulations, which compose nearly all published SRP CFD results found in the literature, are unable to capture or assess. The second phase of this research aimed to perform uncertainty quantification (UQ) for SRP. To begin, a framework has been developed on simpler flows which will be extendable to the SRP flow environment given the best practices for WMLES of SRP revealed by the first part of this work. This UQ framework, entitled NPMs for Nonparametric Probabilistic Method, utilizes Monte Carlo realizations of a hyper-reduced projection based reduced order model (HPROM) to perform both UQ and model updating. This research work has extended the NPM framework to couple with the fluid solver AERO-F, and performed UQ on laminar and turbulent flow over a cylinder. Of greater interest than simply performing UQ is determining what amount of uncertainty is generated by the chosen turbulence model. The turbulence model is of specific interest since for SRP it has been revealed to critically change plume structures and subsequent vehicle loads. Moreover, several studies on the common research model (CRM) have also found errors of >10% in loads attributed solely to the choice in turbulence model. Therefore, this research work performs UQ on the turbulence model by replacing an eddy viscosity model with a hyperparameterized stochastic model that satisfies all realizability conditions and can generate all admissible eddy viscosity models (Spalart-Allmaras, k-epsilon, etc.) and more. Through it’s coupling with the NPM framework, model-updating of the turbulence model’s hyperparameters allows for the discovery of the best turbulence model for a specific problem of interest, while it’s formulation allows for the generation of statistics for UQ on the quantity of interest. This methodology has been successfully applied to NACA 0012 and CRM flow environments, and publications are forthcoming.
