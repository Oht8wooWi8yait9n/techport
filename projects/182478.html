Find me on the Moon: NASA Lunar Navigation Challenge

NASA TechPort Project ID: 182478
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

Develop an aid to orienteering for use by astronauts at the lunar south pole region that does not utilize power, electronics, or software.

Help NASA navigate in and around the lunar south pole by developing a low-tech orienteering device or by creating a method to survey and map the bottom of Shackleton Crater. If you can solve either or both of these challenges, you could win a share of the $50,000 prize purse and maybe your idea will land on the moon.

The goal of the Find Me on the Moon Challenge is to develop novel solutions for orienteering and navigation that can be used by astronauts at the lunar south pole region. Solvers can submit their concepts in one of the two focus areas.


For Challenge 1, NASA is looking for an orienteering tool to help astronauts navigate the lunar surface and return to their spacecraft. If you’re an expert in orienteering without a GPS or a space enthusiast, this challenge is for you. The solution must improve on past devices, offering ease of use, high accuracy, and not rely on highly advanced tech. As an example, Apollo astronauts used a cardstock solar compass, similar to a sundial, and a paper range scale for visual relative distance to the lander.


For Challenge 2, NASA seeks a reliable method to explore and map the Shackleton Crater at the lunar south pole. The solution must handle the harsh conditions, map the crater, analyze its contents, and send data back for future missions. Unlike Challenge 1, these solutions may utilize more advanced technologies, provided they do not depend on satellite-based navigation due to the unique challenges posed by the crater’s geography.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

The "Find Me on the Moon Challenge" provided NASA with diverse, innovative navigation solutions for lunar south pole missions. It validated internal concepts, expanded ideation beyond internal teams, and fostered global participation. Valuable low-tech orienteering and mapping methods were identified, with potential for prototype development and integration into Artemis missions, enhancing astronaut safety and mission success.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Fully successful achieving project goals; solutions planned for future implementation. The project team was impressed with the thoughtfulness and out-of-the-box thinking of many of the submissions, and were very pleased with the interest and participation by contributors from many different countries. The challenge was a great “sanity-check” to make sure NASA wasn’t missing something when brainstorming concepts for navigation at the lunar south pole. The many unique ideas gathered from the challenge went far beyond what our small NASA internal team would have been able to ideate, and the team shared all of the awarded concepts with navigation subject matter experts across the agency. Several submissions that may not have earned an award for directly addressing the challenge, were awarded by the project team for their concept value to the overall lunar surface navigation mission. If resources and schedule allows, the NASA crowdsource initiators plan to produce and test a physical prototype of the top-awarded solution from the low-tech navigation challenge to evaluate its usefulness and potential incorporation into our navigation tools for early Artemis EVAs. This project is a classic example for the benefit of prize competitions. While we could've developed a variety of solutions internally, we would've never been able to generate the broad variety of ideas.
