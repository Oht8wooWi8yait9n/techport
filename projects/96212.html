Minimizing Residual Stresses in Metal Additive Manufacturing using Multiscale and Multiphysics Topology Optimization

NASA TechPort Project ID: 96212
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

The proposed project aims at developing a tool for multiscale, multiphysics, time-dependent problems (TA 11.3.7.1). It is hypothesized that that the overall geometry of structures produced by additive manufacturing (AM), affects the residual stresses. The goal of the tool will be to produce a constrained geometry that minimizes the residual stresses caused by AM, without losing overall stiffness from an applied loading. The method proposed will make use of NASA's micromechanics analysis code (MAC) that implements the generalized method of cells (GMC) to model the structure accounting for the heterogeneous and anisotropic effects of AM at the microscale. At the macroscale, time-dependent heat transfer methods will be used alongside finite element methods (FEM) to quantify the final residual stresses and deformation. A topology optimization (TO) method will then be wrapped around this to optimize the geometry for minimum residual stresses. The tool proposed could be used for analyzing, designing and optimizing structures manufactured with AM methods. It will help mitigate some of the negative side effects of AM (residual stresses) while still maintaining the advantages of AM. The optimized structure should have greater stiffness to weight ratios (critical for flight missions), and due to the minimal residual stresses produced, should be sufficiently strong enough to last longer (resistance to unexpecting cracking and fatigue) than other structures produced by AM methods. These structures may be used as a critical component in space exploration missions. The tool will not be constrained to AM processes but can be generalized to fiber-reinforced composites, and other structures such as metal foams.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Despite the many advantages brought by Metal Additive Manufacturing (AM), often the as-built parts contain high residual stresses and deformations. These side-effects can have adverse consequences on the part’s overall strength and lead to non-conforming parts. This project aims at developing a tool capable of optimizing the geometry of additively manufactured structures to minimizing this residual stress and distortion. The tool would help alleviate some of the negative side effects of AM (residual stresses) while still maintaining the advantages of AM. Optimized structures may have greater stiffness to weight ratios (important for flight applications) and improved resistance to unexpected cracking and fatigue failure (important for critical components). The tool could be used for analyzing, designing, and optimizing structures manufactured with metal AM methods. The tool should be able to optimize the geometry given the parameters of the manufacturing process and any geometrical constraints. This project aims to satisfy Technology Area 11.3.7.1 Sequential Multiscale Analysis Toolset by developing a tool for multiscale, multiphysics, time-dependent simulations. This tool can be developed by first building upon or creating a simulation model to predict the residual stresses and deformations of the as-built parts from the manufacturing parameters and part geometry. The predictions can then be wrapped in a loop to perform the optimization. However, to obtain an accurate prediction of the residual fields the material evolution should be modeled, requiring a multiscale and multiphysics simulation. This in turn requires a material evolution model computing the state and mechanical response of the material at every time step, an inherently multiscale, time-dependent, multiphysics process. Once this simulation model is validated, it should be optimized for computational efficiency to make the optimization iterations feasible. The geometrical optimization can make use of topology optimization methods (TO). The results of the developed simulation are promising. The simulation model is able to reasonably predict the residual stress and final distortion of the part by using a time-dependent thermomechanical finite element simulation. However, this simulation is prohibitively computationally expensive to be wrapped in an optimization loop as-is. More research would be needed to address this, such as using surrogate modeling methods or alternative modeling methods such as the Inherent Strain method (IS). Nonetheless, investigations into the sensitivity of the residual fields with the tool showed that it is feasible to control the residual fields by changing some geometrical parameters. In addition, the tool was used to show that modeling of the material evolution, more specifically the evolution of material porosity during the simulation can have significant effects on the predicted results. In addition, it was shown that the simulation results were highly sensitive to some experimental parameters of the material porosity evolution model used. This project developed a multiscale thermomechanical simulation modeling the powdered bed fusion process in additive manufacturing. The was accomplished with the integration of several open-source tools such as CalculiX, ParaView, and Slic3r, along with NASA’s Micromechanics Analysis Code (MAC-GMC). CalculiX was used to compute the solution at the macroscale while MAC-GMC was used to compute the response at the microscale. This tool was then used to investigate the effects of modeling the evolution of the material’s porosity on the predicted residual stresses. Sensitivity of the residual fields on the geometrical dimensions of a sample part was also investigated. Other contributions to the state of the art include the implementation of a porosity evolution model of the metal selective laser sintering (SLS) process, and an implementation of a super-integration scheme into CalculiX.
