Many-Qubit Arrays for Analog and Digital-Analog Quantum Simulation

NASA TechPort Project ID: 96156
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

In recent years, superconducting qubit technology has advanced to the point that it is becoming useful for noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) applications. Of this, much interest has been directed towards the utility of this platform for analog bosonic and hybrid digital-analog simulation for application-specific Hamiltonians. Superconducting qubit designs for quantum simulation offer advantages over other modalities, such as ion traps and neutral atoms, due to the ease of individual control and the broad tunability of qubit frequencies and couplings [1]. Superconducting circuit-based quantum simulation is also particularly advantageous for open quantum systems as it can be engineered to simulate coupling to a particular environment [2]. Advances in 3D integration of low-loss superconducting circuits compatible with high quality qubits has enabled development of increasingly large 2D transmon arrays [3]. The native coupling of these qubits is extremely well-suited to simulating the Bose-Hubbard model, permitting the study of a large range of mesoscale condensed matter physics. Even small-scale simulations of these systems, on the order of a currently fabricable 6x6 qubit grid, exceed the capabilities of classical computation. Further, these qubit arrays can be used to create multi-photon entangled states when strongly coupled to 1D superconducting waveguides [4]. This entanglement can then be distributed to multiple qubits using artificial giant atoms that allow the qubit-waveguide coupling to be tuned dynamically [5]. The coherent preparation of many-body entangled states in this way may permit simulation of non-local interactions as well as fermionic coupling to bosonic continua.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

The radiation-resistance of graphene combined with the EFLG’s robustness to energetic fluctuation makes these devices strong candidates for computing in space environments, greatly increasing the processing power available for flight computing.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Quantum information processing promises a paradigm shift in our ability to perform complex computations. However, the hardware necessary to do this kind of processing, a fault-tolerant universal quantum computer, is still decades away from practicality. Presently, noisy intermediate scale quantum (NISQ) computers are operational in both academic and industrial research laboratories. These NISQ computers have already been demonstrated to be capable high precision control for a small number of operations or gates. Importantly, these computers can be designed to very efficiently perform specific kinds of computations that leverage their native physics. This approach to quantum computation is typically referred to as analog or digital-analog quantum computing, and it is a promising path towards quantum advantage with current hardware. This project has focused on developing relatively large-scale superconducting quantum processors using digital-analog quantum computing techniques to simulate many-body physics in 2D. Towards this goal, I have designed, calibrated, and demonstrated usability of processors with geometries including a 3x3 square qubit lattice comprising 21 physical qubits and a 4x4 square qubit lattice comprising 16 physical qubits. In order to physically realize these processors, we used superconducting flip-chip fabrication technology developed at MIT Lincoln Laboratory. We demonstrated this technology’s usability for fabricating complex many-qubit quantum processors, as well as its advantages over previous generations of planar technologies. Through this project, we have developed techniques for improving the design, fabrication, and control of quantum hardware. We have also shown that our hardware is a promising platform for studying fundamental physics with our 16-qubit system. Our 21-qubit quantum processor is still in the process of being brought online, and with it, we hope to demonstrate its capability of hosting certain phase transitions expected for our model of superconducting quantum processors. Measurements of this phase transition will be useful both in benchmarking superconducting quantum processors, and in exploring fundamental physics with precision quantum control. We hope that this processor will be a testbed for both MIT and NASA scientists to demonstrate the potential of NISQ technology.
