Technologies to Enable EDL of Smallsat Science and Exploration Payloads

Project Description

The central objective of this research is to develop technologies for Entry, Descent, and Landing (EDL) of cubesat-sized vehicles. While current efforts tend toward landing larger masses, smaller payloads may fulfill scientific and exploration objectives for a fraction of the cost. Difficulties with scalability of existing technologies must be confronted. Previous technology deemed infeasible for larger systems can be considered for low mass architectures. Passive landing systems such as rough landers, for example, are especially appealing due to their lack of required inertial and range sensing close to the surface. The second key aspect of this research is to identify applicability of these systems to a range of missions. This may encompass payload sensitivity to heat and g loads, the landing precision requirements, time requirements, and many more. The primary means of approaching this problem will be a systems-level trade study of several technologies, from conceptual to flight proven. The trade space must include trajectory limitations and vehicle size constraints. Following the conceptual design phase will be experimental testing of selected EDL technologies suitable for smallsat EDL. This work supports NASA's long term goals and interests. Maturation of smallsat technology will provide a more accessible platform for scientific objectives requiring atmospheric entry, such as university sponsored projects. In addition, a low cost and proven means of flight testing small-scale novel EDL concepts will emerge from this research.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: This objective of this research was to develop technologies supporting Entry, Descent, and Landing (EDL) of small vehicles with volumes of 3U (30 x 10 x 10 cm) or less. Efforts in the literature have largely focused on developing high-mass entry systems for human exploration of Mars. However, small EDL systems have unique design characteristics that allow them to attempt challenging atmospheric entry missions with reduced cost and risk. Many scientifically interesting opportunities in the solar system are prohibitive with current EDL technology. Some examples of smallsat EDL applications include flight technology demonstrations, atmospheric probes, sample return missions, and secondary payloads supporting a larger spacecraft. Maturation of technology tailored to small reentry systems enables valuable mission opportunities with accelerated design and implementation timeframes. The overall approach in this study was to develop smallsat design architectures based on efficient scaling and cross-utilization of EDL subsystems. For direct entry missions (terminating at surface impact), it was discovered that coupling of the terminal descent and impact attenuation systems was a severely limiting factor in downsizing heritage technology. A Mars smallsat EDL study found impact attenuation stroke lengths were too large to accommodate standard “stacked” interior configurations while maintaining peak impact loads below 50-100 g’s. A key finding of this study was that packaging constraints preclude minimum-mass configurations. Novel interior configurations were studied in response to this problem, including a toroidal payload layout which was found to alleviate sizing requirements. An impact attenuation study was performed through a collaboration with NASA Ames Research Center. It was discovered that in off-nominal impact scenarios, where the vehicle was oriented at a small angle from vertical, inertial loads were reduced by up to 50%. This behavior was attributed to an effective stroke length increase due to rotation of the external structural components relative to the ground. Kinetic energy was absorbed by bending and shear stresses in the nose plate prior to compression of the crushable structure. This dynamic behavior was validated in LS-DYNA and showed promise to alleviate impact sizing and layout constraints for small vehicles. Hypersonic trajectory performance was studied in the context of smallsat entry vehicles as part of an overall EDL design architecture. Payloads flying secondary to a primary mission must have the capability to compensate for dispersed interface delivery conditions and entry environments to ensure landed accuracy. However, thruster-based active reaction control systems (RCS) were found prohibitive on small vehicles due to internal volume requirements for static hardware and propellant tanks. A study of guidance strategies relevant to smallsat reentry was performed utilizing passive or semi-passive control methods. A study of bank rate modulation at Earth was performed to identify flight regimes for lift nullification. The strategy allowed the vehicle to transition from lifting flight to semi-ballistic flight without adjusting trim angle of attack. This additional degree of freedom can greatly improve the landed accuracy of vehicles with limited control authority. In addition, a trajectory design framework was developed based on optimal bank angle modulation guidance strategies from the literature. A stochastic terminal point guidance strategy was developed to identify optimal control strategies in dispersed atmospheric flight conditions. The method enables discrete lift modulation of small vehicles to achieve precise orbit insertion in aerocapture or precision landing in direct entry missions. This research was a high-level study of the EDL design process for small reentry vehicles characterized by severely restricted interior volumes. Refinements in the design of terminal descent and landing systems were shown to increase impact attenuation capabilities and alleviate internal packaging constraints. A smallsat guidance campaign identified semi-passive strategies to enable high-accuracy hypersonic trajectory performance with limited control authority. The benefits of this work include the rapid design and evaluation of smallsat EDL concepts for otherwise challenging missions throughout our solar system.
