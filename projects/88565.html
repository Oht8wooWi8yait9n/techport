Long-Term Rotational Dynamics of Defunct Earth-Orbiting Satellites

NASA TechPort Project ID: 88565
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio

Project Description

While extensive research has been conducted to predict the trajectories of defunct high altitude satellites, the attitude dynamics of these objects are not well understood. Interestingly, many satellites are known to spin rapidly - with periods as short as several seconds - or have complex rotation states that change over time. My goal is to develop theories explaining and predicting the rotational behavior of defunct satellites in GPS-like and geosynchronous (GEO) orbits. To do this, I will build on previous research involving asteroids. This research suggests that the rotational behavior of defunct satellites is largely due to the Yarkovsky-O'Keefe-Radzievskii-Paddack (YORP) effect, a phenomenon in which solar radiation absorption, re-emission, and reflection produces torques on an orbiting body. In addition to the YORP effect, energy dissipated through structural flexibility or fuel slosh appears to play a key role in the rotation state evolution of defunct satellites. To investigate these effects as well as others, I will develop analytical and numerically integrated simulation models that couple attitude dynamics and orbital motion. These models will include solar radiation pressure (SRP), energy dissipation effects, gravity gradient torques, and n-body influences. To gain insight into the attitude dynamics and validate simulation results, I will collaborate with satellite observers to observe the spin states of particular defunct satellites. The information provided through simulations and observations will allow me to develop theories predicting the rotational behavior of defunct high altitude satellites. Understanding the attitude dynamics of these defunct satellites is important for trajectory prediction, collision risk assessment, and proposed satellite repair and recycling operations. Predicting the long-term orbital motion of these satellites requires accurate modelling of SRP, the strongest non-gravitational force affecting uncontrolled high altitude satellites. Like atmospheric drag, SRP forces are dependent on a satellite's orientation with respect to incoming particles (in this case photons and radiation from the Sun). However, many current orbital debris prediction models neglect attitude considerations when calculating SRP forces, instead assuming constant or at best time averaged SRP coefficients. Incorporating attitude dynamics information into orbital prediction models will yield more accurate pictures of the future orbital environment. Knowledge of a defunct satellite's spin rates will also help predict the potential for satellite break-up or material shedding, allowing operators to navigate critical assets away from these hazards. Finally, satellite repair and recycling operations, which promise to reduce the cost of operating expensive GEO satellites, require repair spacecraft to dock with rotating, uncontrolled satellites. Understanding the satellite spin states that the spacecraft might encounter will be crucial for success. Ultimately, the advancements permitted through an enhanced understanding of defunct satellite attitude dynamics will help protect critical space assets from collisions and ensure the safety of Earth's orbital environment for future users.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Transitioned To (Jet Propulsion Laboratory): Using DSN X-band radar to measure spin states of satellites in near-geosynchronous orbits
  • Closed Out: This research aimed to better understand and predict the long-term spin state evolution of defunct satellites through coordinated observation and dynamical modeling. Improved spin state knowledge is particularly important for active debris removal (ADR) and satellite servicing missions which have to grapple and de-spin these satellites, many of which spin rapidly and weigh several tons. Since these defunct satellites evolve rapidly and are easily observed, they can also provide insight about small asteroid evolution, bodies that are extremely challenging to observe from earth. We focused primarily on satellites in geosynchronous earth orbit (GEO) where solar radiation torques are a primary attitude perturbation. To facilitate study, we observed and modeled the well-documented family of retired GOES 8-12 weather satellites, formerly operated by NOAA. With known geometry and mass properties and a large collection of previous observations showing diverse and highly variable spin states, these satellites were ideal targets for study. Observations of GEO satellites almost exclusively consist of optical data obtained with ground-based telescopes. Given the large distance to GEO (~36,000 km), these satellites only appear as bright dots. While no satellite features can be directly resolved, the resulting brightness vs. time data (known as a light curve) contains significant information about the satellite’s spin state. To better understand how satellite spin states manifest themselves in light curves, particularly for non-principal axis tumbling, we developed a high fidelity light curve simulator with invaluable assistance from Steve Queen at NASA GSFC. With this model we were able to better understand light curve structure and begin detailed analysis of previously collected GOES light curves. Notably, we better constrained the ongoing evolution of GOES 8. This satellite rapidly spun down in 2014 and transitioned to non-principal axis tumbling. Our analysis showed that after this transition, the satellite began spinning back up while tumbling. We also observed a tumbling resonance where GOES 8’s motion repeated periodically even though it was spinning around several axes at once. Analogous resonances are often observed in orbital mechanics, most notably with the asteroid belt and Jupiter’s Galilean moons. To further understand ongoing satellite evolution, particularly with regards to their spin pole motion, we collaborated with Charles Naudet and the radar group at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) to observe the GOES satellites with Doppler radar. Unlike light curves, Doppler radar allows you to resolve satellite features due to the varying radial velocity across the rotating satellite. We developed methods to obtain potentially unambiguous satellite spin pole solutions that require little prior knowledge about the satellite. These GOES Doppler observations showed significant spin pole changes for the satellites. Also the poles of the tumbling satellites were near the sun and anti-sun directions. To our knowledge, these radar and optical studies are the first work to obtain the spin states of retired GEO satellites in non-principal axis tumbling and use them to inform our understanding of long-term evolution. Concurrent to these observations, our research focused primarily on modeling long-term rotational dynamics considering solar radiation torques, internal energy dissipation, and gravity gradient torques. The resulting simulations showed rich, previously unstudied dynamical structure with satellites cycling repeatedly between uniform rotation and tumbling, tracking and precession of spin poles around the sun line, tumbling resonances, and stable tumbling states where solar radiation torques and energy dissipation balance. Many of these behaviors were consistent with the observed GOES evolution. We also found that the end of life solar panel angles of the GOES satellites, which differ considerably, are a primary factor for their observed spin state diversity. This highlighted to potential for properly setting end of life satellite configurations to dictate long-term spin states. Slow, predicable satellite spin rates would greatly aid ADR and satellite servicing. Considering our desire to predict long-term evolution, we developed a new semi-analytical averaged dynamics model that yielded a ~3 order of magnitude decrease in computation time compared to earlier full dynamics models. This averaged model essentially explained the underlying mechanics of the above behaviors and promises to greatly facilitate exploration of long-term defunct satellite rotational dynamics. Overall, our work spanned novel observations, estimation techniques, and dynamical modeling. It uncovered and explored rich dynamical structure that requires much additional research to fully understand. With potentially significant and diverse implications for space debris management and small asteroid evolution, this is a worthwhile endeavor.
