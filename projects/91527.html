Project Description

High data rate deep space optical communication (DSOC) links for manned and unmanned space exploration have been identified by NASA as a critical future capability, enabling advanced instruments, live high definition video feeds, tele-presence, and human exploration of Mars and beyond. Adopting optical communications promises a ten-to-one-hundred-times increase in data rates from deep space for equivalent spacecraft mass and power as compared to state-of-the-art deep-space Ka-band RF communication systems. One of the key enabling technologies for DSOC is a large area photon counting ground detector array. This array has been identified as a top priority in NASAs technology roadmap (as technology area 5.1.1). To minimize the resources consumed on the spacecraft, it is essential to have the best possible single photon counting detectors on the ground. Therefore, the ground detectors should be efficient (a large fraction of the incident photons should be registered as counts) with low jitter (indeterminacy in the photon arrival time), high maximum count rate, and low false count rate. Ground detector arrays capable of supporting Gb/s links from Mars and beyond are within reach, but still require further technological development. To address this need, the Space Technology Mission Directorate Game Changing Development program office has supported a DSOC technology development project at JPL to realize these goals using kilopixel arrays of tungsten silicide superconducting nanowire single photon detectors (WSi SNSPDs). To minimize the mass and power required on the spacecraft, future DSOC links will be encoded using pulse-position modulation (PPM). In PPM schemes, the maximum data rate achievable is ultimately limited by the timing jitter of the ground detector. While some NbN SNSPDs have demonstrated 25 ps jitter, they also have much lower detection efficiency and SNSPDs have not yet been developed which simultaneously achieve near-unity detection efficiency and 10s of picoseconds of jitter. Furthermore, we do not yet understand the fundamental limits to which the jitter of SNSPDs can be reduced. For my doctoral research at MIT, I will investigate the fundamental mechanisms of jitter in SNSPDs and engineer unity efficiency detectors with significantly reduced jitter. This will allow a dramatic increase in the fundamental data-rate limit in DSOC links. A key element of my project plan is numerical simulation coupled to nanoscale characterization. By creating more accurate models for SNSPD behavior and coupling these models to microscopic characterizations of actual nanowires I will be able to understand and then engineer jitter in these devices.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The initial goal of our NSTRF project was to improve the performance of optical communication systems based on superconducting nanowire single photon detectors (SNSPDs) by understanding device physics and improving single pixel performance. SNSPDs show great promise for a variety of classical and quantum communications applications since they can detect single telecom band photons with nearly 100% efficiency, with up to 100s Mhz count rates, and 10s of picoseconds of timing jitter. This work also included integrating SNSPDs with nanocryotrons (nTron) for simple, single layer amplification of the device output signal. When we were partnered with researchers at Goddard, our goals expanded to include improving the performance of kinetic inductance detectors (KIDs), which are a complimentary superconducting detector, well suited for energy resolving applications. Through the course of this research, we integrated WSi SNSPDs with WSi nTrons, made improvements to the NbN material system and used it to demonstrate high performance NbN SNSPDs, fabricated Nb resonators with internal quality factors close to one million, and made progress towards meta-material-like KIDs. We have also studied the thermal system in which SNSPDs ae embedded by studying the IV relations of nanowires on a variety of substrates. Our work included a large number of material characterizations carried out on a variety of materials, especially transmission electron microscope imaging of different preparations of NbN. We also explored the deposition of NbN with a nano-crystalline-like morphology, experimented with hydrogen passivation of disordered NbN, and demonstrated superconducting indium tin oxide by intercalation with sodium.
