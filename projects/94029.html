Assessing the Feasibility of Using Co-electrolysis to Concurrently Convert Carbon Dioxide and Water into Methane and Oxygen for Propellant and Life Support on Mars

NASA TechPort Project ID: 94029
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

By using co-electrolysis with task-specific ionic liquids (TSILs), water from Martian regolith and carbon dioxide from the Martian atmosphere may be simultaneously electrolyzed in an electrochemical cell, producing methane and oxygen in a single process vessel. The following objectives and methods will be undertaken to advance the technology for future crewed missions to Mars. Objective 1 will identify candidate TSILs and electrodes for the cathode compartment of the electrochemical cell. To achieve this, a literature review of potential ionic liquids and cathode materials will be performed in order to identify those suitable for this technology. The next step will be to experimentally verify that they possess desirable characteristics and address knowledge gaps from the literature review. Objective 2 will then evaluate TSILs and electrodes from Objective 1 by characterizing their reaction kinetics, adsorbed species, and intermediates. Rotating disk and rotating ring-disk electrode experiments will be used to study the reduction process of carbon dioxide to methane. The results of these tests will be collectively analyzed to identify and select cathodes and TSILs most conducive to methane production. Objective 3 will focus on designing and fabricating a benchtop-scale electrochemical cell in order to enable the evaluation of selected cathodes and TSILs in a laboratory environment. Objective 4 will characterize the performance of the electrochemical cell with each TSIL and electrode over a range of operating conditions. Candidate components selected at the conclusion of Objective 2 will be integrated into the test article from Objective 3 to parametrically assess the technology on a bench-top scale. The goal is to prove that methane can be selectively produced in relevant operational environments. Objective 5 will assess integrated system-level parameters. The specifications of promising test units, including methane and oxygen production rates, mass, volume and power, will be extrapolated to relevant scales for human spaceflight to enable comparison to functionally similar systems. This research effort is aimed at advancing the technology readiness level of co-electrolysis with TSILs from 2 to 4 by proving the concept of the technology and validating a low-fidelity cell. This will position the technology for further refinement and integration into In-Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU) systems. If an ionic liquid can be identified and implemented in a properly designed electrochemical cell, co-electrolysis should reduce the cost, complexity and risk of crewed Mars missions by simplifying oxygen and methane production to a single chemical step. In fact, an ISRU architecture with this technology would require fewer major processes and components, approximately 50% less mass, and 25% less power than the conventional Sabatier/water electrolysis paradigm for propellant and oxygen production. Acknowledging these advantages and the potential for the technology, NASA calls for the development of co-electrolysis with TSILs in its roadmap for Human Exploration Destination Systems.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Transitioned To: designing a flow cell reactor for future CO2 reduction experiments and analyzing CO2 capture data that I would collect prior to the trip, Also work with contained crew
  • Closed Out: In situ resource utilization (ISRU) is an enabling element of missions to Mars, whereby critical consumables, such as fuel, oxygen, and water, are generated at the surface of the planet prior to the crew leaving Earth. This concept takes advantage of various natural resources on Mars, such as atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) and water (H2O) at the surface. A leading ISRU architecture calls for the use of a Sabatier reactor that operates in parallel with a standalone water electrolyzer to convert CO2 and H2O into methane (CH4) and oxygen (O2) for rocket propellant and life support. Due to the mass of rocket propellant required to launch infrastructure to another planet, however, it is highly desirable to simplify mission hardware and minimize mass, volume, and power wherever possible. Improvements in a bipropellant production plant may be realized using a room temperature ionic liquid (RTIL) to capture CO2 and deliver it to a low temperature co-electrolysis reactor for conversion to CH4 in parallel with water oxidation. To this end, a variety of fundamental bench-top experiments were performed to characterize the viability of the concept. Two RTILs, 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium trifluoromethanesulfonate and 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate, were evaluated for CO2 capture at low partial pressures (≤ 84.1 kPa). Each RTIL was then integrated alongside a copper cathode in a three-electrode H-cell, and the resulting electrochemical performance of each pair was characterized as a function of potential, while oxygen was generated in the adjacent anode compartment. CO2 reduction to CH4 was then demonstrated in a limited proof of concept using a flow cell reactor and supporting experimental apparatus in a more flight-like configuration at a larger scale. Target specifications were then established for a corresponding full-scale system. In short, CO2 reduction to CH4 has been demonstrated with aqueous room temperature ionic liquids in fundamental bench-top experiments, as well as a more flight-like breadboard that was comprised of an integrated CO2 contactor and reactor. As a result of these efforts, it can be argued the TRL of the concept has been advanced from 2 (application formulation) to 3/4 (characteristic proof-of-concept/breadboard validation in a laboratory environment) for spaceflight applications. Results and lessons learned from the laboratory experiments, hardware designs, and the scaled integrated system analysis provide a benchmark for the proposed Mars ISRU architecture and establish performance goals for future research.
