Embedded Normal Form Algorithms for Low-Cost Cislunar Navigation

NASA TechPort Project ID: 186803
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

NASA and international partners are pushing the boundaries of space exploration, increasingly sending missions to complex dynamical environments such as cislunar space and multi-body Jovian systems. This growing complexity, especially with sustained programs like ARTEMIS, necessitates a shift away from simplified models that only consider one or two massive bodies. For decades, trajectory designers have used semi-analytical techniques to understand these environments. However, these techniques typically rely on truncated models that omit complex gravitational perturbations. These unmodeled forces lead to significant trajectory deviations over time, requiring frequent and costly correction maneuvers. I am proposing a method to apply semi-analytical techniques within higher-fidelity models. This approach captures the higher-order interactions and quasi-periodic forcing that define the true orbital environment. By doing so, we gain the analytical insight of traditional methods while providing a framework compatible with high-fidelity mission design. This allows us to map the gravitational scaffolding of a system with high precision and identify natural pathways that are often obscured in simpler models. The methodology involves developing a Quasi-Hill Restricted Four-Body Problem (QHR4BP) toolkit utilizing high-order normal form expansions. This enables the analytical classification of spacecraft motion to determine transit or stable orbit behavior without the iterative numerical integration that typically exhausts onboard processing power. The framework will be developed in Julia to leverage its specialized package ecosystem and ease of rapid development. For flight deployment, these algorithms will be translated into C++ or Rust to align with NASA flight software standards. The research concludes with Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) testing on microcontrollers to benchmark performance in simulated real-time scenarios. This proposal focuses on creating lightweight firmware-ready software optimized for low-SWaP (Size, Weight, and Power) edge computing hardware. Current high-fidelity navigation often requires computationally heavy algorithms that exceed the limits of radiation-hardened flight hardware. My research addresses this by providing near-instantaneous classification of motion. This enables autonomous maneuvering while using significantly less memory than current automatic mission planning methods. Additionally, this firmware will enable increased mission efficiency. In multi-body environments, natural pathways can be leveraged to reduce propellant expenditure. By keeping the research close to the actual dynamics of the system while maintaining the mathematical simplicity required for real-time planning, we can design trajectories that require lower fuel margins. This work directly supports NASA interests in the Lunar Gateway and autonomous cislunar transport. By enabling local high-fidelity planning, we reduce the burden on the Deep Space Network and contribute to mission sustainability by maximizing the utility of available propellant.
