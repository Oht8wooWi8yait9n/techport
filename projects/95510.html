Regolith - Rocket Plume Interaction: In situ measurements to enable multiple landings at the same Planetary Surface Site

NASA TechPort Project ID: 95510
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

This project proposes to develop a prototype sensor package to measure the speed and direction of debris lofted by planetary lander terminal descent engines. The ultimate goal is to develop the technology for a package small enough to be flown as a secondary payload on future robotic landers. Because the physics of ejecta flight is different on the Moon and Mars, a significant innovative aspect of this project is development of technology for a sensor package that could gather needed data at both locations.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

NASA's future human exploration plans call for repeated landings of large vehicles at a single surface site. Such an approach raises the possibility of lander engines creating debris that could harm the lander and surface infrastructure. In-situ ejecta data will be needed to assist in lander design and surface site layout. STMD Technology Maturation support opportunities would be valuable in taking results from commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) based prototype hardware and experience gained from using this prototype hardware in ground test stands to develop flight hardware of significantly reduced mass and power usage such that it can be on small robotic landers, such as the smaller range of proposed Commercial Lunar Payload Service (CLPS) landers or Mars landers of the sized used for the recent Insight mission.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The goal of this Center Innovation Fund (CIF) project; Regolith – Rocket Plume Interaction: In situ measurements to enable multiple landings at the same Planetary Surface Site, is to develop a sensor package capable of measuring in situ characteristics of terminal descent engine interactions with planetary surface material. Ideally, this package is small enough to fly as a secondary payload on future robotic landers. In situ data from such a package is important because human missions to the surface of the Moon or Mars will rely on landers of significant size. Terminal descent engines are likely to loft a significant amount of debris (both object size and total quantity) 10’s to 100’s of meters from the landing site, as observed during Apollo landings and simulated during Constellation Program-era research. This debris poses a potential hazard to the lander itself and to infrastructure located nearby. In-situ data are needed to calibrate and validate computer simulations necessary for lander design and surface base layout. This project completed the first year of a two-year project plan. The project developed (a) a white paper summarizing key characteristics of this plume-surface interaction (along with a bibliography of supporting research), (b) a set of sensor package requirements to guide future development, (c) a sensor technology survey and evaluation relative to the sensor package requirements, and (d) identification of two sensors requiring additional development that would have been carried out in the second year.
