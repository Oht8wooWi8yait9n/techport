Optically Coupled Laser and Ultra-cold Particle Beam for Breakthrough In-space Propulsion

NASA TechPort Project ID: 118392
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

Current forms of beamed propulsion, while ideal for deep space missions, suffer from diffraction of the light over long distances thus lowering thrust. Additionally, the energy efficiency of photon momentum transfer is small compared to that of particles with mass. The innovative in-space propulsion concept proposed here utilizes a laser beam coupled with a jet of ultra-cold (µK) atoms to eliminate diffraction and self-guide over vast distances. The optically coupled system gives the benefit of efficient momentum transfer through the atoms traveling at high speed and maintaining beam intensity over millions of kilometers. This new capability enables spacecraft on deep space missions to reach tremendously high velocities, at or above 4% the speed of light in several cases under evaluation. The key parameter of this propulsion method is the ratio of the atomic mass flux to atomic temperature, also known as the brightness. Maximizing the atomic beam brightness enables missions with high velocity and larger payload. In this work, a ground-based, high brightness, laser-cooled atomic source will be developed and characterized using optical diagnostic methods. The beam parameters will be analyzed using laser induced fluorescence (LIF) and absorption spectroscopy. These measurements will provide an estimate of atomic temperature, beam density, and bulk velocity, resulting in the calculated brightness of the atomic source. After characterizing the beam, existing laser-particle coupled simulations can be validated by spatially overlapping a high-intensity tunable laser near 780nm to investigate optical coupling phenomena. The primary benefit of this propulsion system is the increased payload mass capacity and reduced mission time, thus providing the opportunity to explore new destinations, even those as distant as Proxima Centauri.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Exploration missions to distant celestial bodies and neighbor stars require significant advances in space propulsion systems. An ideal candidate for these missions is directed energy propulsion, concepts in which an emitter is housed on or near Earth and directs energy (commonly in the form of photons or particles) at the science spacecraft to transfer momentum. Laser and particle beam directed energy concepts suffer from inherent divergence of their beams during propagation, thus lowering the effective thrust on the science spacecraft and limiting mission scope. This research seeks to address the beam divergence limitation by spatially overlapping a laser and neutral rubidium atom beam to produce a self-guiding combined beam that offers significantly reduced divergence. By tuning the overlapped laser beam off the rubidium atomic resonance, a dipole force can be produced that causes that atoms in the beam to concentrate in regions of higher laser intensity. The higher atomic concentration yields a greater index of refraction which, in turn, guides the overlapped laser. Under this grant, ultrahigh vacuum facilities were developed to house an experiment to generate and study the overlapped beam. Additionally, tunable diode laser absorption spectroscopy was implemented to study the influence of the laser on the atom beam’s divergence without impeding the flow of atoms. A method for decoupling the combined beam was developed to evaluate the atoms’ influence on the overlapped laser by comparing the final refracted laser profile with its free-space propagated profile. While work remains to develop a more capable rubidium atom source, the facilities have been tested by extracting the propagated laser profile and by quantifying rubidium atomic density at multiple locations in the propagation.
