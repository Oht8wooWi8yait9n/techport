Project Description

The future of Mars exploration will require landing vehicles much larger than anything humans have ever attempted. This challengedrives a need for a new entry and descent technique: Supersonic Retropropulsion (SRP), the use of firing rockets to reduce speedwhile still in the upper atmosphere. Due to the difficulty to test Mars SRP systems on Earth, NASA is relying on computational (CFD)simulations to help motivate design decisions for future entry and descent vehicles. A large multi-center team at NASA has spentthe better part of the last decade developing cutting edge CFD simulations of SRP flow fields. While many significant findings haveresulted from the studies, the results have exposed many gaps in current turbulence modeling techniques that will need to be addressedbefore results can be used to design full-scale SRP vehicles.This project proposes to innovate and expand on modern CFD techniques to model high energy flow structures. The existingcomputational tools implemented by NASA and industry are based on solvers that were never intended to handle the highlycompressible flows found in SRP systems. Due to this, most published SRP solutions show high uncertainties or inconclusive resultsin the highly turbulent regions. We plan to bridge modern high energy physics capturing techniques only conceptualized in academiawith current NASA solvers in a way that increases the confidence in CFD solutions of SRP flow fields so that they can be a valuable,reliable design tool for future Mars vehicle design.Ultimately, our project will benefit NASA and the high-speed flow community as a whole by developing and applying novel techniquesto SRP flow problems. In doing so, we will achieve physically accurate data to aid in vehicle design for NASA, but also generatevaluable data-sets for the high-speed flow research community.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Previous Mars landings have relied on the original Viking architecture for entry and descent. Over forty years, evolutionary improvements to the rigid aeroshell and supersonic parachute framework have been made, but the general method for entry and descent has remained the same. For future exploration missions, payloads will reach masses of 10+ metric tons, an order of magnitude larger than any previous payload sent to Mars, and a new generation of entry, descent, and landing systems is required [1]. NASA has identified supersonic retropropulsion (SRP) as the ideal entry and descent method for high mass payloads to Mars [2-4]. The largest hurtle to develop these systems is generating meaningful data of SRP flow fields [5-7]. NASA will rely on CFD techniques alongside test campaigns to develop future mission architectures and predict vehicle performance at Mars. NASA has demonstrated highly unsteady turbulent flow, which propagates large oscillatory moments and axial loads on the entry vehicle which pose a challenge for the guidance and control [4]. In addition, recent studies explain that limitations in spatial resolution and state-of-the-art solvers lead to difficulty in predicting how the physics of SRP flow structures impact the vehicle [6]. The goals of this proposal are to use innovative turbulence modeling techniques developed at the CRoCCo Lab to assess the dominant flow physics in SRP and to augment NASA’s understanding of SRP flows by including state of the art, physics-based unsteady turbulence modeling for highly accurate and efficient predictions. This project will help to raise the overall confidence level in CFD simulations of SRP such that it can better suit the NASA teams charged with designing SRP entry and descent vehicles in the near-future. This project is broken down into three phases. First, we reduced the time to solution of existing high-bandwidth resolving numerical simulations by implementing a novel curvilinear adaptive mesh refinement for direct numerical simulation (DNS) and wall-resolved large eddy simulations (WRLES). This allows us to run the existing CRoCCo code in large-eddy simulation mode with significantly reduced grid sizes, thus leading to faster turnaround of SRP simulations [8-9]. Second, in the next phase of the project, we will validate the resulting high-fidelity (HiFi) data against the existing SRP tunnel-test data, i.e. experiments from both 2010 UPWT [4] and the 2023 Propulsive Descent tests at LaRC [3]. Finally, in the third phase, we will provide a report on the key physics relevant to both the tunnel-like and flight-like SRP conditions with an assessment of current tools employed to simulate retropropulsion environments and what previous modeling assumptions may or may not hold true in key regions of interest. Much of the work aims to significantly reduce the current uncertainty in NASA simulations and to enable physics-based HiFi solutions for complex SRP geometries in timelines that are practical to NASA programs. Thus, a strong focus of this effort is the assessment of the time-to-solution reduction of the new integrated techniques and the accuracy of the novel simulation data.
