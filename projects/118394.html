Variable Conductance Radiator for CO2 Deposition in Deep Space Transit

NASA TechPort Project ID: 118394
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

A key technology required for deep space, long-duration human exploration missions is the CO2 removal as part of life support and environmental control. Current state-of-the-art, solid sorbent-based CO2 removal technology has reliability and capability gaps demanding alternative solutions for future exploration missions. A promising CO2 removal technology under consideration is the CO2 deposition method that creates cold surfaces and cools cabin air to such low temperatures that CO2 is deposited onto the surface. However, this process results in high power consumption. As Mars missions provide a capability to view deep space at environmental temperature of ~4K, utilizing radiators for heat rejection is emerging as an opportunity to enable CO2 deposition. The goal of the proposed research is to investigate and demonstrate the feasibility of an innovative thermal radiator, Variable Conductance Radiator (VCR), that can modulate its heat rejection and leverage cold sink conditions of deep space to deposit CO2 from a gas stream as part of the spacecraft air revitalization system. The research plan involves specific objectives of: (i) Design, modeling, and optimization of the VCR; (ii) Development of a functional prototypical VCR system; (iii) Experimental characterization of the VCR and validation of the modeling; and (iv) Predictions of performance and scalability of the overall VCR subsystem. The proposed research will provide much-needed insights and framework for a reliable, modulated, energy-efficient heat rejection technology to enable further development of the CO2 deposition system, and also to facilitate new approaches for other applications such as thermal management and in-situ resource utilization.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: CO2 removal technologies are crucial to support human exploration of space. Although the ISS has been using solid sorbents (Zeolites) to capture CO2 for years, NASA currently seeks to replace solid sorbents as they require large volumes of material for absorption capacity, high power usage, and degrade over time. Liquid sorbents may be used as an alternative to solid sorbents and offer simplicity leading to reliability; they are estimated to consume 65% less power, weight, and volume than solid based CO2 scrubbers. In 2018, NASA began investigating liquid amine-based technology for CO2 capture, however when applied to space applications, this technology depends on an effective gas-liquid separation method under microgravity conditions. This work investigated Vortex Phase Separator (VPS) technology as a new approach for a liquid amine CO2 removal system. VPS technology applied to CO2 removal allows for liquid and gas phases to make direct, efficient contact by tangentially injecting gas (high CO2 stream) and liquid (liquid amine) through two nozzles on a fixed, right circular cylinder. This unique design also allows for gas and liquid phases to simultaneously, effectively separate, due to buoyancy-driven forces. The design of the VPS follows a physics-based methodology used to analyze gas phase bubbles, which occur as gas and liquid phases coalesce. An experimental setup was built using both fabricated and commercial components. Testing of the experimental system allowed for the characterization of input parameters such as CO2 flow rate, temperature, and humidity. Data was then analyzed to evaluate the performance of VPS-based CO2 removal technology. The results obtained in this work characterize the VPS system for CO2 removal in terms of various operating parameters for gas and liquid phases, as well as provide initial insights into how a VPS sized for stability and applied to CO2 removal performs. The subscale design for a VPS with a 3” inner diameter, 3.5” outer diameter, and 3.63” height removed a maximum of 84% of CO2 from a CO2 stream at 3.7 SCFH flow rate, 14°C temperature, and 82% RH, using 100 mL of 100% amine circulated at 1.52 LPM flow rate. The designed VPS also showed to be effective in removing relative humidity of the CO2 stream by up to 26% for the stated parameters. This work demonstrates VPS technology as an effective, alternative CO2 removal technology that could be scaled and used to support human exploration of space.
