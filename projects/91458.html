Developing an Adaptive Robotic Assistant for Close-Proximity Human-Robot Interaction in Space Environments

Project Description

As mankind continues making strides in space exploration and associated technologies, the frequency, duration, and complexity of human space exploration missions will continually increase in the years to come. Whether it is a return to the lunar surface, exploring an asteroid, or visiting Mars and its two moons, efficient collaboration between astronauts and robotic systems will be essential to the success and efficiency of future human missions. Improvements in human-robot interfacing and integration can also have substantial near-term benefits, as several new robotic system such as the Dextre, Robonaut 2, and Smart SPHERES have been deployed in the International Space Station in the last five years. The central objective of the proposed research is to enhance human-robot interaction in robotic systems in space in order to unlock the full potential of currently deployed robotic platforms as well as to benefit human space exploration missions in the future. In support of achieving this objective, the specific aims and proposed implementation methods of the research are: 1. Design a safety system capable of ensuring safe and comfortable human-robot interaction by monitoring various parameters such as separation distance, closing rate, and the huma's vision field in order to intelligently adjust the robot's motions. 2. Develop an intent recognition capability based on behavioral models which utilize task-level knowledge and observable parameters such as gaze direction, posture, gestures, and speech to actively predict astronaut intent and respond accordingly. 3. Validate the efficacy of the developed systems, both under nominal conditions as well as with localization uncertainty, through simulation and experimentation by utilizing quantitative measures of human-robot fluency, efficiency, and safety. This research is most closely related to the objectives stated in NASA's Robotics, Tele-Robotics and Autonomous Systems Space Technology Roadmap, in particular the Human-Systems Integration section. Within this section, the Safety, Trust, & Interfacing of Robotic/Human Proximity Operations sub-section describes the need for safe physical interactions, which is highly relevant to the first aim of this research. The Intent Recognition & Reaction sub-section, on the other hand, describes the importance of recognizing astronaut intent and reacting to it in an intelligent way, which is relevant to the second aim.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The overall goal of the research performed under this grant was to improve the quality of human-robot interaction through human motion prediction with application to both space and terrestrial environments. More specifically, the objective was to develop a technique that allows for accurate and robust prediction of motion in a variety of environments and scenarios without the need for expert knowledge and handcrafting of task-specific predictors. The specific applications explored include interaction with a free-flying robot (NASA’s Astrobee) onboard the International Space Station (ISS), co-navigation with a mobile robot in a factory setting, and collaboration with a robotic manipulator on a tabletop task. These scenarios and environments differ in many key qualities, such as the types of motions observed and the sensors used for human detection and tracking, making the development of a robust predictor challenging. To address this difficulty, we developed a general framework for combining multiple complementary prediction approaches called the Multiple-Predictor System (MPS). This data-driven technique compares the relative performances of component prediction methods at various time horizons of interest in order to construct a high-performing predictor tuned specifically for the given scenario. Namely, using motion and task data of a task split into “training” and “model selection” subsets, the MPS chooses the best predictors for the given scenario and automatically tunes their parameters without the need for expert input. A diagram of the framework is depicted in We evaluated the performance of the MPS against three implemented individual prediction methods: a velocity-based position projection, an extension of a time series prediction technique developed by Perez D’Arpino and Shah [1], and an extension of sequence prediction by Letham et al [2]. In the task of predicting future human positions at various time horizons of interest, the evaluation showed that our approach, compared to the above individual methods, reduces prediction error by 18.5%, 28.9%, and 37.3%, respectively. Furthermore, by varying parameters such as the order and number of human motions in the task, we were able to show that the MPS can adapt to various conditions by changing the composition of the combined predictor for each scenario. The MPS consistently outperformed the individual, baseline predictors, showing the robustness of our approach. For more details, please see the related publication titled "A Multiple-Predictor Approach to Human Motion Prediction,” published in the proceedings of the 2017 IEEE International Conference on Robotics and Automation (ICRA) [3]. In order to understand the effect of using the MPS to inform robot behavior in human-robot co-navigation, we implemented a proof-of-concept demonstration in a test factory environment containing a mobile robot with single-axis mobility . In the demonstration, we integrated the MPS with a robot motion planner that reasons not only about what positions the human will occupy in the future, but also at what specific instances in time (i.e., planning in time) . The goal of the demonstration was to show the MPS’s ability of providing predictions in real time and to assess the impact of a system combining these predictions with planning in time on the quality of human-robot co-navigation. We compared the resulting robot behavior of our approach with that of two baseline methods. The first baseline is the current state-of-the-art in safety systems designed for factory settings in which the robot stops whenever a person enters the area of the work cell that the robot can physically reach (i.e., the shared region). The second baseline uses planning without prediction, where the motion planner only considers the person’s current position and continuously replans as new positions are received. Through the implemented proof-of-concept demonstration, we were able to qualitatively show that combining human motion prediction with planning in time results in improved human-robot co-navigation. Namely, when using our approach, the robot exhibited anticipatory behaviors such as yielding the way to an approaching person. Importantly, these behaviors are derived automatically from applying our method, without the need to explicitly program them in advance. Figure 2 demonstrates the benefit of such anticipatory behavior. A video comparing our approach to the baselines can be viewed at the following URL: http://tiny.cc/cobotSAM. To our knowledge, this was the first demonstration combining human motion prediction with optimal robot motion planning in time implemented on a factory-certified robotic system. Figure 2: Stills from the test factory environment demonstration. The robot’s task is to navigate to the other side of the linear axis, while a human attempts to go to the depot. The black line parallel to the rail denotes the boundary of the shared region. Note the difference in the robot’s position on the linear axis (yellow arrow) between the start (a) and when the human arrives at the depot for each of the three implemented methods: factory safety system baseline (b), planning without prediction baseline (c), and our approach (d). Instead of stopping close to the depot (b) or blocking the human’s path (c), our approach results in an anticipatory, yielding behavior (d). While the test factory environment demonstration showed qualitative improvements in co-navigation between the human and robot, the setup is not fully representative of how a robotic assistant would be deployed in a real factory. In a real deployment, a single robot would service multiple workstations in a larger area. Therefore, we decided to perform a more thorough, quantitative analysis in a larger, simulated factory work cell (10m x 3.1m floorplan with the robot’s rail being 10m long). In order to improve the fidelity of the simulations, we collected real human motion data of four distinct motions using a motion capture system and used it to simulate human motion. We ran a set of 30 simulations using each of the two baselines described above as well as using our method. In each simulation run, the robot executed a given set of tasks while the human performed a random permutation of eight actions. Through the results of the simulations, we found that our approach, when compared to the baselines, led to fewer safety stop triggers, less human idle time, less unplanned robot idle time (i.e., robot idle time caused by the safety stop system) and shorter human and robot task times. As a result, we were able to show that using our approach of combining human motion prediction with planning in time led to safer and more efficient human-robot co-navigation. For more details, please see the related publication in the Robotics and Automation Letters (RA-L) in a paper titled “Human-Aware Robotic Assistant for Collaborative Assembly: Integrating Human Motion Prediction With Planning in Time” [4]. While we were able to reduce prediction error with the MPS, many human motion prediction approaches, including the implemented components of the MPS, rely on partial trajectory alignment in order to provide accurate predictions. In this context, partial trajectory alignment is the problem of finding a suitable point on a representative trajectory that aligns with the last point of a given partially observed trajectory of the same type of motion. Prior approaches to solving this problem often struggle with trajectories that have overlapping segments, regions where the human stops for a period of time, and significant variance in execution speed. Furthermore, given a full and partial trajectory, prior techniques only provide a single alignment that maximizes some cost function, while a probability distribution over possible alignments is desirable. In order to overcome these drawbacks, we designed a novel partial trajectory alignment technique that leverages a set of previously observed motions in order to build a Bayesian model that provides a distribution over potential alignments. Our technique combines a mixture of optimization, supervised learning, and unsupervised learning components. This includes forming an accurate representative trajectory for each distinct motion type, implementing unsupervised detection of stop segments within the representative trajectories, utilizing an optimization-based segmentation method that separates overlapping regions into different segments, training an online segment classifier, building a set of temporal prior distributions, and defining a distance-based likelihood for each segment. We evaluated our method against two state-of-the-art approaches, online dynamic time warping (ODTW) and optimal sequence bijection (OSB), as well as a baseline method from our prior work that selects the nearest point in a moving window (MW). The evaluations were done on three distinct datasets: reaching motions in a tabletop factory task, walking motions within a factory work cell (described above), and analogue astronaut motions. The results indicate that, in terms of alignment error in seconds, our method performs either as well as or better than the highest performing baseline for each dataset. Across all the datasets, our approach reduced the alignment error by 65.9%, 49.9%, and 26.4% when compared to the ODTW, OSB, and MW methods, respectively. The analogue astronaut motions used to evaluate our partial trajectory alignment technique were collected through collaboration with the Intelligent Robotics Group at NASA Ames Research Center (ARC). We collected these motions using Astrobee, a free-flying robot developed by NASA designed to operate inside the International Space Station. In order to simulate astronaut motion, we utilized a specialized test bed in the Granite Lab at ARC. We recorded human motions with Astrobee’s onboard sensors, including a fisheye lens camera and a depth sensor. We also developed a method for human detection and tracking suitable for use in the lab environment. In the context of applying our work in space environments, using Astrobee’s sensors improves the fidelity of the proof-of-concept implementation and testing of the developed techniques. In summary, the objective of the work performed under this grant was to develop a robust and accurate method of human motion prediction in support of improving human-robot interaction and co-navigation in space and terrestrial environments. Toward this goal, we developed a novel, data-driven technique called the Multiple Predictor System that combines complementary prediction methods in order to construct a robust, high-performing predictor without the need for expert input. Next, we investigated the effect of combining human motion prediction with planning in time on human-robot co-navigation. We were able to demonstrate anticipatory robot behaviors in a test factory environment and, through evaluations in simulation, showed that our method leads to quantitative improvements in safety and efficiency. Finally, in support of further improving human motion prediction, we developed a novel technique for partial trajectory alignment. This technique allows for computing a distribution over potential alignments based on Bayesian inference and is composed of several optimization, supervised learning, and unsupervised learning components. In the future, we aim to investigate the impact of the improved partial trajectory alignment on prediction performance and, in turn, the quality of human-robot co-navigation.
