Demonstrating SWIR performance of SLS detectors and compact optical designs for Earth science applications

NASA TechPort Project ID: 105694
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

Goddard is a leader in thermal instrument design, building on innovative detectors such as the Strained Layer Superlattice (SLS), with proven performance from 3.3 – 10.7 µm on the Compact Thermal Imager (CTI). Here, we propose to test the capability of existing SLS detectors for imaging in the shortwave infrared (SWIR), including the 2.1-2.3 µm atmospheric window covered by instruments such as the Landsat Operational Land Imager (OLI), and develop optical designs for SLS focal plane arrays capable of imaging across this extended spectral domain (2 – 12 µm) with a wide field of view and compact form factor.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

The potential for a single SLS-based instrument to cover the broad 2 – 12 µm range would support future land imaging missions, including follow-on opportunities that build on CTI, and NASA's longstanding commitment to the Landsat Program.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The project successfully completed all proposed testing and design objectives. Two strained layer superlattice (SLS) detectors were tested for their quantum efficiency in the shortwave infrared (SWIR) spectral domain. Multiple optical designs were developed to match the broad spectral coverage and focal plane array configurations for Earth science applications using SLS-based imagers. These advances pave the way for instrument designs with more robust imaging performance and broader application for Earth science. The purpose of the Goddard Space Flight Center’s Internal Research and Development (IRAD) program is to support new technology development and to address scientific challenges. Each year, Principal Investigators (PIs) submit IRAD proposals and compete for funding for their development projects. Goddard’s IRAD program supports eight Lines of Business: Astrophysics; Communications and Navigation; Cross-Cutting Technology and Capabilities; Earth Science; Heliophysics; Planetary Science; Science Small Satellites Technology; and Suborbital Platforms and Range Services. Task progress is evaluated twice a year at the Mid-term IRAD review and the end of the year. When the funding period has ended, the PIs compete again for IRAD funding or seek new sources of development and research funding, or agree to external partnerships and collaborations. In some cases, when the development work has reached the appropriate Technology Readiness Level (TRL) level, the product is integrated into an actual NASA mission or used to support other government agencies. The technology may also be licensed out to the industry. The completion of a project does not necessarily indicate that the development work has stopped. The work could potentially continue in the future as a follow-on IRAD; or be used in collaboration or partnership with Academia, Industry, and other Government Agencies.
