Kinetic Inductance Detectors for Far-Infrared Spectroscopy

Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio

Project Description

I propose to do research to develop kinetic inductance detectors (KIDs) as a tool for far-infrared spectroscopy. Incorporating KIDs as the key detector technology of a sub-orbital or satellite mission to observe the sky at far-infrared wavelengths will allow for highly sensitive extragalactic spectroscopy, leading to new insights in galaxy formation and evolution. Specifically, I propose to develop KID technology to allow for improved sensitivity and larger detector arrays for long-slit, direct-detection spectroscopy.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Kinetic inductance detectors (KIDs) are small superconducting resonant circuits patterned out of thin metal films. Each detector pixel has an element capable of absorbing incident radiation. Absorbed radiation breaks Cooper pairs in the superconducting film into quasiparticles, which in turn change the resonance frequency and linewidth of the resonator. The resonator is coupled to a feedline in order to measure these resonance shifts. To form an array, each pixel can be tuned to have its own resonance frequency. It is therefore possible to distinguish the signals of many KIDs coupled onto the same transmission line. KIDs are an extremely versatile technology, as they can be designed to detect and respond to incident radiation at many wavelengths across the electromagnetic spectrum, from x-ray to microwave. For this investigation, I focused on developing KIDs for spectroscopy of astrophysical sources in the far-infrared wavelength regime. Tracing the history of early galaxies by studying their spectral properties is an important aspect of modern astrophysics. Many of the galaxies present during the time when the universe was undergoing its most rapid rate of star formation (at a redshift of around 2) are heavily enshrouded with dust, which obscures measurements at optical wavelengths. There are also a number of spectral lines present in the far-infrared that serve as useful tracers of star formation activity and the properties of the interstellar medium. The observational technique of intensity mapping is a particularly useful way to extract information about the aggregate spectral properties of galaxies present at a given redshift, and does not require targeting or detecting individual galaxies. Current state-of-the-art for spectral measurements in the far-infrared is the Atacama Large Millimeter Array, a direct-detection interferometer. Though ALMA is able to achieve high sensitivity and spatial resolution, it would be far too time-intensive to use it as a large-area mapping instrument. By contrast, KIDs are a promising technology to use for spectral intensity mapping measurements in the far-infrared for several reasons. Most importantly, they can be designed to achieve the high sensitivities necessary to detect the far-infrared line emission and continuum radiation from early galaxies. They can also be highly multiplexed, which allows for relatively simple cryogenic readout electronics schemes in instruments that use them. Transition-edge sensors, the current state-of the-art for focal plane instruments, are highly sensitive but require complex cryogenic readout schemes. Over the course of this investigation, I have worked on the design, fabrication, and testing of kinetic inductance detectors targeted for application on a balloon-borne far-infrared spectrometer instrument. I have demonstrated a 45-pixel array of lumped-element KIDs fabricated out of 40- nm aluminum film on a silicon substrate, and integrated it with a multitone FPGA-based readout system.
