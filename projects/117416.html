Pulsar Navigation for Crewed Exploration of the Solar System, Year 3

NASA TechPort Project ID: 117416
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

Future crewed and uncrewed exploration vehicles will be operating in environments far removed from the Earth and will be required to operate and navigate with minimal support from the ground. The use of millisecond x-ray pulsars as a source of timing and navigation is being demonstrated in LEO with SExTANT/NICER. The capability of these systems needs to be matured in preparation for the Evolvable Mars Campaign (EMC) missions. At the very least, an x-ray navigation sensor can be used to complement optical navigation as an accurate timing source for Mars and Earth entry. This sensor has the added benefit of aiding the astrophysics (x-ray astronomy) community in getting science outside of LEO.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

The Gateway station will need to be able to navigate autonomously without relying on the DSN and the ground to determine its navigation state. x-ray navigation, along with optical navigation will provide a navigation capability that will be essential for the Evolvable Mars Campaign. This technology addresses two key requirements: accurate navigation and autonomy.All future architectures for crewed exploration of the Solar System will require the vehicles such as Gateway to operate autonomously for periods of time without relying on the DSN and the ground to determine its navigation state. In particular, the delays due to one-way light time may be up to several minutes. These vehicles will be required to return the crew to Earth in case of loss of communications with the ground. A central aspect of this is the ability to navigate independent of the ground.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The goal of the Pulsar Navigation for Crewed Exploration of the Solar System project was to mature the X-ray/Pulsar Navigation technology for use in crewed missions which will explore the Solar System beyond the Earth-Moon system. One of the major challenges in developing a system which will operate far from planetary bodies is to maintain an accurate navigation state which will allow the vehicle to reach its goal (or equivalently to return the crew to Earth) in the case of loss of communications with the ground. To that end, x-ray navigation is a perfect complement to optical navigation which will allow the vehicle to navigate autonomously to achieve the mission objectives. The challenges addressed in this project was to mature both the hardware and the understanding of how a crewed vehicle can use x-ray navigation outside of Low Earth Orbit (LEO). In the past two years Near Rectilinear Halo Orbit (NRHO) trajectories were used to evaluate the performance of a single x-ray sensor. The results showed that the attitude requirements of using a single x-ray sensor are rather tight; having more than one x-ray sensor working together, either pointed in the same direction or in a different direction will allow for more opportunities to obtain measurements without placing undue burden on the attitude control system. We initiated conversations with Gateway to use it as a platform to mature the hardware and to determine the performance of such a sensor system in cis-lunar space away from Earth.
