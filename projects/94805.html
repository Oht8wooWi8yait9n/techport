Project Description

The Next Generation Micro-Shutter Array (NGMSA) is a programmable slit mask designed to enable space-based multi-object spectroscopy. It is an evolution of the JWST micro-shutter technology and is transitioning from APRA to SAT program development for TRL maturation to level 5 in support of four science instruments in three Decadal Survey mission concept studies, 1 Explorer mission concept, and a sounding rocket program. The APRA-funded development of the NGMSA focused exclusively on eliminating the magnetic actuation and was successful on 128 x 64 pixel format pilot arrays. However, it utilized the JWST-era MEMS manufacturing process for which our ability to achieve recurrent manufacturability is low. We have severe process yield problems to resolve. We have designed a MEMS architecture change that is simpler to fabricate than the JWST-era process that we are currently struggling with and intended to use for the proposed SAT program. The technical objective of this IRAD project is to prototype this new MEMS architecture in order to achieve recurrent manufacturability. This IRAD work is critical to the success of the subsequent SAT effort to bring large (840 x 420 pixel) format arrays to TRL-5 for five science instrument applications that have baselined it. We believe that our ability to successfully perform the SAT-proposed work will depend critically on prototyping the process change described below on a small 128 x 64 pixel pilot arrays using IRAD resources. This IRAD request is not duplicative of any other proposal. It reflects breakthrough thinking that was not appreciated when the SAT proposal was written.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

The NGMSA is an enabling technology for several Decadal Survey missions.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: We have developed an automated actuation test system combining three motorized axes of stages, probe card and imaging optics to characterize microshutter arrays in bare die form. This system allows for simultaneously making multiple electrical contacts on the contact pads of an unpackaged microshutter array to actuate individual rows of a microshutter array. With the built-in imaging system, we were able to evaluate how this microshutter array operates without any packaging. This new capability saves valuable resources, including labor cost, packaging materials, PCBs, electronic components, and most importantly, time, to screen and select microshutter arrays for further testing and packaging. The purpose of the Goddard Space Flight Center’s Internal Research and Development (IRAD) program is to support new technology development and to address scientific challenges. Each year, Principal Investigators (PIs) submit IRAD proposals and compete for funding for their development projects. Goddard’s IRAD program supports eight Lines of Business: Astrophysics; Communications and Navigation; Cross-Cutting Technology and Capabilities; Earth Science; Heliophysics; Planetary Science; Science Small Satellites Technology; and Suborbital Platforms and Range Services. Task progress is evaluated twice a year at the Mid-term IRAD review and the end of the year. When the funding period has ended, the PIs compete again for IRAD funding or seek new sources of development and research funding, or agree to external partnerships and collaborations. In some cases, when the development work has reached the appropriate Technology Readiness Level (TRL) level, the product is integrated into an actual NASA mission or used to support other government agencies. The technology may also be licensed out to the industry. The completion of a project does not necessarily indicate that the development work has stopped. The work could potentially continue in the future as a follow-on IRAD; or be used in collaboration or partnership with Academia, Industry, and other Government Agencies.
