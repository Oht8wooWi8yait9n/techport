A low-SWaP timing system for distributed in-orbit and planetary surface platforms

Project Description

Low-cost missions in cislunar space demand positioning, navigation, and timing (PNT) and communications capabilities that are not achieved with current onboard timing systems. No space-rated atomic standard fits the size, power, and stability requirements for these applications. Accordingly, this work addresses the technology gap in precise timing systems for small spacecraft operating in the cislunar domain. This work proposes the investigation of an ensemble of compact atomic clocks onboard and distributed across in-orbit and planetary surface platforms. Such an ensemble would realize the timing stability of the best clock across the linked platforms at medium-term to long-term averaging intervals. Furthermore, the overall reliability of the timing signal will increase, reducing its sensitivity to environmental perturbations seen by the individual clocks. Particularly, the impact of the ensemble in a lunar PNT satellite system will inform the performance of the navigation system. The ability to steer a local reference oscillator on the platforms directly to this ensemble time will alleviate the burden of ground monitoring and broadcast correction updates traditionally utilized in Global Navigation Satellite Systems. Therefore, this work will have critical impacts to the size, weight, power, cost, and precision of lunar PNT systems. The key contributions of this investigation will be (1) a computer-based model equipped with optimal ensemble algorithms and informed with data gathered through lunar mission datasets and hardware testing, and (2) a preliminary testbed to evaluate ensemble behavior emulating dynamical and environmental impacts. Methods proposed in this work involve periodic corrections from weak GNSS signals, clock comparison via pulsed optical and RF intersatellite links (ISLs), and corrections for special and general relativistic effects. Algorithm development, simulation analyses, and hardware testing will be conducted. The plethora of datasets now available from lunar missions, such as LuGRE, will inform realistic error models for this work.
