In situ characterization of subsurface cracking behavior in SiC/SiC composites

NASA TechPort Project ID: 96209
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

Ceramic matrix composites (CMCs) have excellent mechanical properties and thermal stability. This makes them excellent candidates for structures in spacecraft, which regularly see extreme operating conditions. However, there is a knowledge gap concerning the subsurface crack behavior in these materials. This severely limits the proliferation of CMCs into high impact areas. Many current studies of cracking behavior are conducted at a free surface, which is not indicative of bulk behavior. This is primarily due to lack of characterization tools that can observe subsurface damage. Recent advances in X-ray computed tomography (XCT) have greatly enhanced resolution, allowing for multi-scale imaging of bulk material. Here, I propose making use of these recent advances in XCT, coupled with novel machine learning (ML) techniques, to simultaneously develop effective characterization tools and probe crack behavior in high radiation, high temperature environments. This study will result in the development of a general characterization technique and fundamental insight into failure of CMCs, which NASA engineers can then use to design against it.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Fiber-reinforced ceramic matrix composites (CMCs) are a class of lightweight, damage tolerant, refractory structural materials for extreme environments. Their design consists of an array of ceramic fibers connected to a ceramic matrix via a weak interphase (Figure 1). Such design allows for substantial damage tolerance in the form of crack deflection and arrest, which allows engineers to leverage the desirable properties of monolithic ceramics while preventing catastrophic failure. As a result, CMCs have been incorporated into the hot section of turbine engines as high pressure temperature vanes, shrouds, and combustor liners, and are being considered for fuel cladding in generation IV nuclear reactors. Their desirable properties and the safety critical nature of their application spaces necessitates a detailed understanding of how CMCs respond to thermo-mechanical and environmental loads.In recent years, acoustic emission (AE) has become a popular method for investigating CMC mechanical behavior. AE is uniquely powerful because it allows researchers to triangulate damage source, identify areas of concern in a structure, and evaluate the severity of subsurface damage in real time using a relatively inexpensive (<$10,000) array of piezoelectric transducers. Despite its power, the ability to distinguish between damage mechanisms, such as damage to the matrix or to the load-bearing fibers, just from the acoustic signals has remained an elusive goal in CMC systems. The objectives of this research are two-fold: 1) leverage novel machine learning (ML) techniques to develop the capability for damage mechanism identification in CMCs, and 2) characterize the robustness of these techniques so that they can be used to study mechanical behavior in safety-critical materials and structures. Towards these goals, the spectral framework has been created. This is the first AE-ML framework capable of damage mechanism identification in CMCs (Figure 3) and represents a significant advancement in damage monitoring capabilities. Its performance has been benchmarked against state-of-the-art AE-ML frameworks and has been found to offer substantial gains in performance. Furthermore, the spectral framework has been used in conjunction with x-ray computed tomography to develop a supervised ML framework, capable of real-time damage mechanism identification. While these tools are in the early stages of technology readiness level, they are sufficiently robust for use in scientific investigations, and further development will permit use in structural health monitoring.
