Investigation of Particle Effects on a Hypersonic Mars Entry

NASA TechPort Project ID: 96211
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

Several successful missions have previously traveled to Mars and been very useful for furthering human understanding of the planet. However, missions in the future will require larger landed masses and decreased uncertainties, especially if a manned mission is being attempted. Therefore, improving current computational tools will be important for meeting these requirements. One event of interest that occurs on Mars is the formation of planet encircling dust storms; these storms sweep dust high into the atmosphere, which could interfere with an entering vehicle’s heat shield. The reasons for the formation of these storms is not well understood, so it not realistic to predict when they will occur and therefore it is vital to characterize how these particles interact with an entry vehicle. Due to the complicated aerothermodynamic nature of the hypersonic flow field, it is challenging to understand the effect these particles will have. Experiments have been performed to investigate these effects; although they show that the addition of particles causes an increase in both heat flux and mass loss of the thermal protection system material, they cannot recreate many of the conditions that are present for a realistic Martian entry. Based on this, it would be desirable to have numerical models that help in the understanding of particle laden hypersonic flows. The goal of the proposed research is to investigate some of these interactions between the particle and the flow field and to model these effects in a way that is useful for current computational tools. As a first step, interactions between the particle and the flow field that are not currently accounted for will be investigated to determine their importance. Based on the small particle size, a useful tool for analysis is this stage is the direct simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) method. Some of the effects to be considered are particle-shock interactions, particle drag and heat transfer models, phase change of heated particles, and particle interactions with fibrous thermal protection system materials. Then, the effects that are deemed to have a non-negligible impact need to be modeled. Finally, the new models will need to be added to existing computational flid dynamics (CFD) tools. These tools can then be used to more accurately determine the effect atmospheric particulates have.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Over the fellowship period, research focused on improving particle modeling relevant to hypersonic Martian entries through a large dust storm and then characterizing the impact these models had on the quantities of interest (i.e. TPS damage caused by particle impacts). Specifically, a new particle drag model was developed that accounts for differences in gas type and an efficient method for inter-particle collisions (which may be necessary for a large number of simulated particles) was investigated. While inter-particle collisions are likely to play more of a role at higher particle concentrations expected in experimental facilities rather than at flight-relevant conditions, the effect of several underlying particle models (including particle drag) on the particle-induced TPS erosion was estimated throughout the Mars 2020 entry trajectory via a sensitivity study. This sensitivity study consisted of performing several Lagrangian particle-tracking simulations with different underlying particle modeling at several free-stream conditions. The main outcome of this study was that underlying particle drag and heating models do not significantly affect particle-induced erosion, whereas the size distribution of free-stream particles significantly affects particle-induced erosion. While several previous studies have used a similar Lagrangian particle-tracking methodology and sensitivity studies have been performed at a few relevant conditions, the current work was the first effort to systematically determine the effect of underlying particle modeling for an entire Mars entry trajectory. The previously mentioned drag model and stochastic particle-particle collision models have been implemented and verified in the DUst Simulation and Tracking (DUST) Lagrangian particle-tracking code developed at NASA Ames. Additionally, the drag model has been utilized in a project funded by the Office of Naval Research (ONR) for understanding how stratospheric particulates in Earth's atmosphere could potentially affect TPS degradation or roughening.
