Calcium-based Battery Development for Space Technology Applications

NASA TechPort Project ID: 118452
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio

Project Description

Mobile energy storage devices are essential to the success of exploratory missions in space. When astronauts return to the moon and eventually travel to Mars, it is crucial that the portable high-specific-energy devices they depend on for extravehicular activity will be unerringly reliable and safe. As the current designs for intercalation-based lithium-ion batteries near their performance limits, it has become clear that new and innovative approaches to energy storage are needed. A compelling, potentially viable alternative is calcium-based batteries, which offer high theoretical energy densities, promising safety features, and novel electrochemical conversion reactions that could support the next generation of manned space missions. The field of calcium-based batteries is an exciting and relatively unexplored research area with a low technology readiness level. The goal of this proposal is to engineer a calcium metal electrode that, if successful, will enable rechargeable, high-specific-energy calcium-based conversion batteries. To achieve this, the first objective is to design and create a stable synthetic surface film on a dry calcium electrode that can enable reversible calcium reduction, unlocking the rechargeable functionality. The second objective is to examine the electrochemical properties of the synthetic calcium electrode in a conversion-based battery, focusing the battery metrics for space technology applications. This project will utilize research facilities at MIT that allow for controlled chemical reactions in inert environments, as well as electrochemical testing. The project will also utilize analysis facilities available at MIT to examine film morphology and chemical composition. Collaboration with NASA engineers will be an important component throughout the scope of the proposal to deploy a more informed approach to the electrode design process.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Batteries based on calcium (Ca) metal electrodes offer the possibility of high energy density, safe operation, and an alleviation of supply chain concerns compared to batteries based on lithium metal electrodes. However, only a handful of electrolytes have been discovered that allow for reversible Ca electrodeposition and electrodissolution (i.e., plating/stripping), which is needed for the operation of a rechargeable Ca battery. The primary objective of this project was to engineer a Ca metal electrode and electrolyte system that enabled reversible Ca plating/stripping in order to promote the technological development of Ca-based batteries. The first research approach was to design and test a series of exemplar solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layers on dry Ca electrodes to examine whether plating/stripping activity could be decoupled from the intrinsic electrochemistry of the baseline electrolyte, Ca(BH4)2 in tetrahydrofuran (THF). The cycling behavior of three distinct surface films — a pre-existing oxygen-rich interface, a chemically-formed native interface, and a synthetic CaF2 interface — was examined on Ca foil electrodes using cyclic voltammetry. Regardless of the interface composition, initial cycles exhibited interface-dominated electrochemical features while later cycles exhibited electrolyte-governed plating/stripping behavior. Thus, ensuing research efforts focused on electrolyte engineering. The second research approach was to systematically alter the Ca2+ coordination environment in the electrolyte to modulate Ca electrochemistry and SEI formation. Anion-based respeciation was first demonstrated as an electrolyte modification technique. Ca(TFSI)2, a salt reported to deactivate Ca metal in THF, was mixed into the baseline electrolyte Ca(BH4)2/THF, varying the proportion of the two anions. The energetically favorable nature of Ca2+ BH4- interactions compared to Ca2+--TFSI- prompted the BH4- anions to displace TFSI- from the Ca2+ coordination environments on average. This enabled Ca plating activity when the concentration of BH4 was larger than that of TFSI-; however, the chemically-passivating nature of TFSI- suppressed subsequent Ca stripping. Solvent-based respeciation was next examined using glyme co-solvents. Glymes displaced THF from the Ca2+ coordination environment and weakened Ca2+ BH4- interactions. As glyme proportion increased, electrochemically-formed SEI (i.e., SEI formed during Ca deposition) comprised increasing amounts of solvent-derived C-O species and decreasing amounts of salt-derived borate species, whereas the compositions of chemically-formed SEI (i.e., SEI formed from Ca foil soaked in electrolyte) were approximately constant regardless of electrolyte formulation. The co-coordination of diglyme and BH4-, two species reported in reversible Ca electrolytes, led to Ca passivation with continued cycling, indicating that the Ca2+ coordination environment mechanistically impacts the Ca deposition process. The main contributions of this research are new techniques and design principles for the modification of Ca electrochemistry in a tightly constrained electrolyte design space. Results from this project have led to the publication of three journal articles and one patent. These studies have delineated future directions for Ca electrolyte development that focus on the composition and configuration of the Ca2+ coordination environment. This in turn has facilitated the technological development of Ca-based batteries
