Micro-Scale Gallium Nitride Pressure Sensors for Advanced Harsh Environment Space Technology

NASA TechPort Project ID: 91572
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

The goal of this research is to study the high-temperature response of the 2-dimesional electron gas (2DEG) that occurs at the interface of aluminum gallium nitride (AlGaN) and gallium nitride (GaN) upon mechanical deformation. These studies will enable a new platform for sensing mechanical strains (e.g. pressure) within extreme harsh environments. Sensing within hostile environments will further the understanding of space environments and advance space technology. The proposed work will contribute to investigations of planetary atmospheric profiles like that of Venus, to analyzing the material properties of aircraft structures when in hypersonic flight, and to allow sensing within rocket propulsion systems. Additionally, the use of GaN as the material platform for sensor development can reduce spacecraft payloads by eliminating the addition of complex packaging because GaN is temperature tolerant, chemically resistant and radiation hardened. This research supports NASAs technical research areas such as TA12 (materials that can survive the space environment and lightweight structures with reduced packaging) and TA10 (Sensors, electronic and devices, miniature instruments) as described in the Space Technology Roadmap (STR). Currently, most commercial sensors are made from silicon because it allows simple integration into integrated circuits (ICs) and their fabrication process. However, silicon cannot be used for sensors within harsh environments, which can include high temperatures, abrasive chemicals, large radiation exposure, and/or high accelerations. The electrical properties of silicon degrade at temperatures above 150°C due to the generation of thermal carriers. Interest is wide bandgap materials is thus of interest for sensing within high temperature and other hostile environments. One promising material for these applications is gallium nitride (GaN) with a bandgap of 3.4 eV compared to that of Silicon at 1.12 eV, which enable of operation of devices at temperatures greater than 600°C. Additionally, GaN is radiation hardened, chemically inert, and mechanically stable due to strong interatomic bonding, 8.92 eV/atom compared to 2.34 eV/atom for silicon. GaN is a promising material for the design of a platform for harsh environment sensing because, in addition to the ability to operate in hostile environments, GaN is highly piezoelectric. When AlGaN and GaN are interfaced, a phenomenon known as the 2DEG effect occurs. AlGaN and GaN are both Wurtzite crystals, but their crystal lattice dimensions vary, which generate different spontaneous polarization within the materials; upon stacking these two materials, their lattice mismatch creates a positive fixed charge at the interface. The positive charge then attracts mobile electrons to the interface, creating what is known as a 2DEG layer. This work will leverage the 2DEG effect that occurs in AlGaN/GaN heterostructure to design sensors that are electrically sensitive to mechanical strain with in the device. This work will focus on the design and microfabrication of pressure sensors that utilize the change in polarization at the interface of AlGaN/GaN when subjected to strain. However, this research will also lay the ground work for other harsh environment physical sensors like accelerometers, gyroscopes, skin friction and strain sensors. Finally, this work will attempt to characterize and model the properties of the 2DEG layer at the interface of AlGaN and GaN to better understand this unique phenomenon in order to optimize sensors design.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The goal of this work was to design, microfabricate and characterize a temperature-tolerant, pressure sensor to support NASA’s future space exploration missions, specifically an in-situ Venus lander. More specifically, AlGaN/GaN and InAlN/GaN heterostructures were explored as the material platforms for the development of a robust and miniature pressure sensors. GaN is wide bandgap material, allowing operation in extreme temperatures up to 1000°C. This is important for the development of harsh environment micro-sensors and electronics because silicon electronics fail by 300°C. Additionally, another advantage of utilizing GaN as a material platform is that most GaN electronic devices rely on a 2-dimensional electron gas (2DEG). The 2DEG is a highly conductive sheet of electron that forms partially because of a piezoelectric polarization. This means that the 2DEG is strain sensitive and is can be leveraged for pressure sensors. A model was built to compare how the sensitivity to pressure was effected by different barrier layer alloys (InAlN or AlGaN) and their aluminum concentration within the HEMT. The model uses Kirkoff-Love plate theory to model a clamped edge diaphragm and an analytical model of the 2DEG carrier concentration. Figure 1a plots the percent change of the 2DEG and the difference in 2DEG concentration as a function of aluminum content for the device at the edge of diaphragm under 4 psig of pressure. The modeled diaphragm is 500 µm in radius and is 3 µm thick. The model shows that InAlN/GaN heterostructures, in comparison to AlGaN/ GaN heterostructures, have a larger response to pressure because AlN has a larger piezoelectric response than GaN and because InAlN HEMTs have high AlN content. The change in magnitude of 2DEG concentration is 3.5 times larger for an In0.17Al0.83N/GaN diaphragm compared to an Al0.25Ga0.75N/GaN diaphragm. However, because InAlN also has a larger nominal 2DEG concentration, the percent change of the 2DEG is only 33% larger than AlGaN. InAlN/GaN-on-silicon pressure sensors were microfabricated leveraging ring-shaped HEMTs at the edge of a diapghrams. A 500-µm radius InAlN/GaN pressure sensor was experimentally characterized at room temperature from 0 to 28.5 psig. The maximum change in current per pressure (i.e. sensitivity) demonstrated was 0.64%/psig at VGS = −5 V, VDS = 2.2 V. Sensitivity is plotted as function of gate voltage in Figure 2a and shows increased sensitivity as the device approaches the threshold voltage. Additionally, the current–voltage (ID–VDS) response of a released device and an unreleased device was measured up to 550°C on a high temperature probe station to observe the influence of temperatures and strain. A subset of the data is shown in Figure 1c. The solid-state device was more conductive than the released device, at all temperatures. This is because when the device is released the thin film tensile-stress between the underlying GaN buffer layers and the silicon decreases and reduces the piezoelectric effect the 2DEG experiences. This work demonstrates the feasibility of using InAlN/GaN sensor architectures for high temperature space applications. Additionally, time based pressure measurements and pulsed based ID-VDS pressure measurements of a 500-µm radius AlGaN/GaN HEMTs were completed. Results showed repeatability and recovery was bias dependent and time dependent. The performance was effected by trapping and detrapping of surfaces states. It is widely accepted that traps affect the device performance and the degradation of GaN HEMTs. In terms of performance, current collapse, drain-lag, gate-lag, and current slump are all thought to be caused by electron and hole traps in the device. In addition to the demonstrating the need to carefully bias HEMT base sensors, this work illustrates that the reliability of the HEMT for electronics is susceptible to stress conditions. Over the course of this fellowship, GaN pressure sensors were microfabricated and characterized under various bias, high temperature conditions. These research activities lay the groundwork for the development of additional GaN-based sensors (e.g., temperature, acceleration and angular rotation) and electronics, as well as provided a training experience for the graduate student. Additionally, the work demonstrates the ability of GaN sensors to operate in high temperature environments and motivates continued research in the field.
