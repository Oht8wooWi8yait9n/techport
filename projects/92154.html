Certification of Additive Manufacturing Processing Parameters through Physics-Based Predictive Simulation of Process-Defects-Microstructure

Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio

Project Description

Additive manufacturing (AM), also known as 3D printing, is a next generation technology for cost-effective and accelerated way of manufacturing complex-shaped metallic components by successively adding and melting layers of alloy powder directly controlled by a 3D computer-aided design dataset. It has many critical applications in NASA missions such as those found in propulsion and spacecraft. Prior to acceptance of AM processes by NASA, other government agency, and commercial space missions, however, a longstanding technical challenge is the qualification and certification of AM processes and systems for critical, high-value metallic components. The main objective of the proposed research is to develop a physics-based, predictive modeling approach for a rigorous yet efficiency way of certification of AM processing parameters, alloy composition, and resulting microstructures for the desired properties of metallic components via Laser-Powder Bed Fusion (L-PBF) AM process. Particularly, an integrated process-microstructure models will make it attainable for significantly new capable for multiple laser passes, drastically improving the relevance of process-microstructure models to actual AM production scenario.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

Significantly new capable for multiple laser passes, drastically improving the relevance of process microstructure models to actual AM production scenario. Foundation to enable predictive modeling based approaches to certify both the L-PBF and allied AM processes as well as the resulting AM-manufactured flight hardware.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: Additive manufacturing (AM) is the process of building components up incrementally through material addition. It has the potential to realize significant cost and schedule reductions in manufacturing complex components in mission critical applications. Approaches to certify both the AM process as well as the resulting AM-manufactured flight hardware are needed for future NASA, other government agency, and commercial space missions. AM process modeling of predictive relationships between the processing parameters, material composition, and resulting microstructure is a critical enabler to AM certification. Due to the complex physical phenomena in AM, many simplifications had to be used in the physics-based models, limiting their predictive capabilities. For example, many existing models assumed idealized powder particles having spherical shape whereas the real particles exhibit some degree of non-sphericities. Additionally, many existing microstructure evolution models relied on temperature fields calculated by solving only heat conduction equation while ignoring the important effect of molten metal convection on temperature distribution. The objective of thisearly stage innovation research is to improve the understanding of material-processing-defect-microstructure relationship in selective laser melting. This is done via significantly improving the models’ predictive capabilities in powder packing structure on the powder bed, molten pool dynamics, micro-segregation due to non-equilibrium solidification, and solidification texture. In this research, two advanced physics-based, predictive models have been developed and integrated for significantly new capabilities of predicting process-microstructure relationship, with a focus on Nickel Alloy IN718. Specifically, for AM processing modeling, we used discrete element method to calculate the powder particle packing configuration involving non-spherical particles. The computational fluid dynamics method with free surface flow was then used to calculate the molten pool dynamics and the resulting temperature conditions, surface finish and defects during AM. For microstructure modeling, computational thermodynamical simulation was used to calculate micro-segregation of alloying elements during non-equilibrium solidification, and kinetic simulation was used to predict precipitation kinetics during rapid cooling locally within the local chemistry variation caused by the micro-segregation. Lastly, a new computer program implementing the phase field method was developed to calculate texture evolution. For technology transitions, this phase field model has been shared with our NASA collaborators for evaluation. These physics-based models are relevant to the needs for developing a rigorous methodology toward certifying additively manufactured parts built via selective laser melting for future space vehicles. In particular, the melt pool and defect modeling could have near term applicability to parameter optimization. Computational prediction of microstructures can be directly related to mechanical properties and will help to reduce the amount of mechanical testing required. The process-microstructure simulations provide a rigorous foundation for developing know-hows for mapping desired part properties back to optimal process parameter settings, which can greatly benefit the use of additively manufactured parts in future space missions.
