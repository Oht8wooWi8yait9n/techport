Hot Structures Advanced Lightweight Powder Metallurgy (HALP-ACO)

Project Description

Lockheed Martin (LM) Space is currently investigating advanced high temperature materials for heat shields and supersonic/hypersonic hot structures. LM will perform and collaborate with NASA Langley Research Center (LaRC) on testing and characterization activities focused on advanced materials. LM will provide to LaRC powder metallurgy and metal matrix composite (MMC) materials for metallurgical examination and mechanical property testing. The MMC materials will undergo friction stir processing both to modify microstructure and as potential manufacturing methods. LaRC will characterize and test both feedstock and the produced products in order to characterize the effect of processing on microstructures and measure post-processed material properties. Additionally, LaRC will investigate non-traditional methods for estimating processing induced improvements in material ductility, including nanoindentation and flexure testing.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

Lightweight high-temperature powder metallurgy materials fabricated by solid state friction processing offer improved specific strength and stiffness which translate into reduced vehicle mass. Reduced vehicle mass enables greater mass allocation for payloads and support systems. Successful technology development will be extremely beneficial for the advancement of affordable, lightweight materials for hypersonic and Entry, Descent, and Landing (EDL) applications, enhanced defense systems, and commercial spacecraft. The partnership between LM and NASA will help advance high-temperature materials for both commercial and government space applications. NASA will benefit from metallic thermal protection systems that cost less to qualify and can be produced more efficiently in small or large quantities.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The Hot Structures Advanced Lightweight Powder Metallurgy ACO (HALP-ACO) evaluated advanced high temperature materials for elevated temperature structural applications in supersonic/hypersonic systems such as heat shields for Entry Descent and Landing vehicles and hot structures in hypersonic systems. The approach in this study was to fabricate and characterize powder metallurgy (PM)-based metal matrix composite (MMC) materials using solid-state friction-based processing methods. The PM-based MMC materials of interest offer potentially lighter weight, high performance alternatives to ingot metallurgy titanium and Inconel materials. Solid-state, friction-based processing offers a lower cost alternative to traditional manufacturing methods such as forging, rolling, and fusion-based additive manufacturing (AM). Additionally, solid-state processing can be applied to materials that are not compatible with fusion-based AM. Through collaboration between Lockheed Martin (LM) and NASA Langley Research Center (LaRC) under this Announcement of Collaboration Opportunity (ACO), candidate PM-based MMC materials were identified and down selected for fabrication using friction stir-based processing methods. The materials of interest were titanium and aluminum matrix MMCs with nanoscale reinforcements and unreinforced TiAl, produced as processing feedstocks. The solid-state processing methods used in this study were friction extrusion by the shear assisted processing and extrusion (ShAPE) process and additive friction stir (AFS) deposition. The goal was to improve material strength and stiffness by developing a uniform distribution of nanoscale reinforcement particles while also retaining ductility at levels sufficient for structural applications. The material processed by ShAPE friction extrusion was AA2024 reinforced with 3% strontium titanate (SrTiO3), which may offer benefits in intermediate temperature structural locations on supersonic and hypersonic vehicles. Materials processed by AFS were gamma titanium aluminide (gTiAl) and Ti6242 reinforced with 3% yttria stabilized zirconia (YSZ). Characterization and mechanical property testing was performed on feedstocks for the AFS and ShAPE process and post-processed materials. Evaluation included tensile testing at room and elevated temperatures, fracture toughness testing at RT, and 4-point bend testing at RT. Metallurgical characterization included light and scanning electron microscopy, analysis of material composition, and hardness evaluation. PM-based MMCs and TiAl generally have low ductility (<5%) compared with unreinforced wrought counterparts. Measuring ductility can be challenging using traditional tensile testing due to difficulties extracting test specimens from small volumes of material and inherent material notch sensitivity. Another focus of this ACO was to explore alternative methods to estimate ductility and detect changes in ductility with processing. Instrumented indentation (nanoindentation) was explored to determine whether a qualitative correlation could be established between traditionally measured ductility and nanoindentation parameters. 4-point bend testing evaluated whether failure strains in bending provided an estimate of tensile ductility. Nanoindentation was found to correlate with material strength but not with ductility. A correlation was established between strains to failure in tension and bending for ductility levels below 2%. The combined materials/processing technologies evaluated in this ACO are considered a TRL 3 because fabrication of the materials and the processing methods have been demonstrated at laboratory scale. It is anticipated that these technologies will be advanced to a TRL 4 through demonstration of material fabrication and improved post-processing properties.
