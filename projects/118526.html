Development of In-Situ Concrete Materials for Lunar Infrastructure

NASA TechPort Project ID: 118526
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

The Artemis program is about to start a new era of space exploration and a new chapter of human history. As part of this program, a sustained human presence on the Moon is a goal. The Moon, however, presents a variety of challenges that must be overcome by materials in lunar infrastructure for such a goal to be possible, like extreme temperatures, reduced gravity, and little to no atmosphere to protect from solar radiation. Concrete materials are one of the most plausible options as they can combat these issues as well as can be formed into unique shapes, be 3D printed, and provide resistance to the extreme environment. Here it is proposed that a lunar regolith geopolymer concrete be studied on the International Space Station. The designed mixtures maximize the amount of in-situ materials and minimize the need to transport materials from Earth. It is desired, however not required, that these samples be mixed and then placed within the Techshot Multi-use Variable-gravity Platform (MVP) to solidify at the Moon’s gravity level in order to obtain a proper understanding of the material for its intended end goal. After the samples return to Earth, a microstructural analysis using techniques such as scanning electron microscopy, x-ray diffraction, and nanoindentation will be used to understand the microstructural properties and try and deduce an understanding of mechanical performance. This project coincides with NASA’s Technology Roadmaps and specifically the materials section (12.1). By the end of this endeavor, it is envisioned that a material suitable and designed with maximum in-situ lunar resources will be formulated for use within the Artemis program to enable a sustained human presence on the Moon.

A material suitable and designed with maximum in-situ lunar resources will be formulated for use within the Artemis program to enable a sustained human presence on the Moon.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The overall objective of this research was to develop and advance the use of in-situ lunar concrete materials to construct infrastructure that will enable a human presence on the Moon. The Artemis program is starting a new era of human space exploration and a new chapter of history. The Moon, however, presents a variety of challenges that must be overcome, including extreme temperatures, reduced gravity, and little to no atmosphere to protect from solar radiation and vacuum effects. A fundamental goal was to understand how the lunar regolith can be used within a geopolymer concrete. The designed mixtures maximize the amount of in-situ materials and minimize the need to transport materials from Earth. The mixtures also require less water than traditional concrete formulations and the water is released throughout the solidification reaction allowing for potential recollection and reuse. This project coincides with the 2020 NASA Technology Taxonomy materials area (12.1), as well as the in-situ resource utilization area (7.1). As part of this work, studies were conducted on the ground and the International Space Station addressing five major objectives. The first objective was to understand whether gravity had any influence on the micromechanical properties of cementitious lunar regolith simulant composites that solidified at variable gravities on the International Space Station (ISS). The second objective was to characterize multiple lunar regolith simulants, with some designed to represent the same type of regolith on the Moon, for use in geopolymer lunar concrete. The third objective was to understand the influence of vacuum environment conditions and the relationship between the geopolymer mixture design and the curing environment. The fourth objective focused on facilitating a rapid cure for geopolymer lunar concrete through microwave radiation. The last objective was to understand the durability of geopolymer lunar concrete from exposure outside the ISS and a subscale simulated rocket engine test.
