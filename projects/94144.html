Space Debris Threat Mitigation via characterization of strain rate effects by Maximum Entropy Atomistics

NASA TechPort Project ID: 94144
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

Space Debris and micrometeoroid threat mitigation poses a significant and growing future challenge to virtually all NASA missions. As such, there is a direct need to understand the mechanics and physics of any such impact events. However there is an inherent difficulty in understanding strain rate effects using small scale physics model due to the extremely short timescales involved when considering atomic motion. We propose a methodology, max-ent atomistics, which takes into account in an average sense the extremely short timescales and allows prediction of atomic motion over much longer timescales, namely those of interest in impact threat mitigation.

Anticipated Benefits & Mission Applications

This methodology allows prediction of atomic motion over much longer timescales, namely those of interest in impact threat mitigation. This could be useful for space debris and micrometeoroid threat mitigation.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: One omnipresent material response regime in NASA missions is that of high strain rates due to high velocity impacts of orbital debris or micrometeoroids. The analysis of the physics of these systems is complex, requiring accurate understanding processes at multiple length and timescales. We study methodologies for modeling the rate dependent deformation of solid materials: specifically, we examine max-ent atomistics and related extensions to study systems with long timescales and commensurate lower strain rates. We take, as an example of these methodologies, the study of deformation in amorphous silica glass at high pressure and strain rates. We show that these methodologies can successfully predict the material-scale behavior. Remarkably, with the material characterization in hand, we are able to predict microstructure formation in the form of shear banding which plays a critical role continuum macroscale deformation processes and is intimately parameterized by the materials state properties including the rate of deformation. This line of inquiry provided both insights into both fundamental mechanics and provides a template for future analysis to be incorporated into the practical design of space structures for any material. My hope is that such future analyses enable improved designs and mission success. Additionally, I had the opportunity to pursue complementary research during my visits to the NASA Ames. Within the space community, there has been growing interest in reuse of heat shields and re-entry after extended operations in space (for instance Mars Sample Return). At the same time, concern has increased regarding the rapidly increasing population of space debris in near earth orbit and corresponding increased probability of an impacted mission asset. Within the theatre of re-entry and descent, these two issues couple to motivate a critical question: If an impact event occurs, will the re-entry be able to proceed with a high confidence of success? We pursued a physics-based understanding of material damage in these scenarios. Towards this goal, we proposed a cohesive law for the thermal protection material PICA that is micro-mechanically motivated and validated its behavior against data reported in the literature.
