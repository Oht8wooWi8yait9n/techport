Learning the Electron Density Fields Predicted by Density Functional Theory Using Physics-Inspired Machine Learning Approaches

NASA TechPort Project ID: 96152
Official NASA Source: Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) TechPort Portfolio
View Official Project on NASA TechPort →

Project Description

Density functional theory (DFT) is one of the common simulation methods used in understanding the feasibility of the potential atomic structures for various chemical compounds. Unfortunately, DFT methods suffer from poor scaling of computational cost with simulation size, limiting both the types of physical phenomena and number of systems that can be studied. To overcome these limitations, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a promising solution. Here, I propose a novel machine learning approach to connect the atomic structure to its DFT predicted electron density field. The ML approach will employ a combination of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) as well as the recently developed Materials Knowledge System (MKS) framework at Georgia Tech (GT). The advantage of learning electron density is twofold – it can be used to directly derive other DFT outputs such as energy and atomic forces, and also to dramatically reduce computation time by serving as an excellent starting point for DFT calculations on completely new atomic structures. There has been limited research on using ML in this context. The advantage of the proposed approach is that it will aim to combine the best attributes of CNN and MKS methods. Typical CNN models suffer from a vast parameter space and ‘black box’ nature that makes model optimization a difficult time-consuming process and restricts our ability to extract physical intuition from the results. The MKS framework overcomes these challenges by casting the governing physics in the forms of Green’s function derived convolution kernels, which can guide the development, optimization and interpretation of CNN models. To demonstrate the significance of the proposed ML model, I identify two potential applications relevant to Technology Area 12.1 “Materials”: the prediction of DFT interatomic forces for efficient and accurate molecular dynamics simulations, and the rapid discovery and simulation of novel multifunctional materials with tailored properties.

Technology Transition & Outcomes

  • Closed Out: The goal of this work was to study interactions between humans and complex systems (systems that have multiple humans, robots, software agents, interfaces, interaction modalities, etc.) that are relevant to space exploration. Specifically, our work focuses on how interactions among and across the components of an integrated system influence the way humans perceive, understand, communicate, and work with such a system. This grant supported research that resulted in findings about (a) how public-facing service robots can use agents that have different kinds of relationships to their users to provide personalized experiences during multi-person interactions, (b) how humans use language to mourn robots, notably the Mars Opportunity Rover, in social media posts, (c) how people perceive trust and competence of a multi-robot system after a failure is experienced, based on the strategy the system uses to perform its recovery from such a failure, and (d) current use patterns and envisioned future use of smart home technologies and human-agent interactions in smart Page 2 – Revised 02/2018 environments. It has also supported (e) the development of a conceptual framework for understanding human-smart environment interactions, (f) the development of a conceptual model of AI agent as mediators in relationships among individuals, embodiments, and services, and (g) studies that used design research methods to understand people’s perceptions of robots and IoT devices that interact with one another in present-day and future smart environments. These projects have resulted in several conference and journal publications (ACM/IEEE Human-Robot Interaction Conference (HRI); ACM Human-Agent Interaction Conference (HAI); ACM Conference on Designing Interactive Systems (DIS); IEEE Transactions on Human-Machine Systems Journal (THMS)) and actionable design guidelines for natural and conversational human-agent interaction in complex, integrated, multi-device systems.
